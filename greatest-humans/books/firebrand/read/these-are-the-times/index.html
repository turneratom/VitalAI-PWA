These Are the Times

Crisis papers and the winter of a cause

The retreat began in darkness, with the river making a black wound through the November cold.

Thomas Paine stood above the landing at Fort Lee and watched an army attempt to disappear. Men came down the bluff bent under muskets, kettles, blankets, cartridge boxes, and the invisible weight of knowing they had been outmaneuvered again. Wheels struck stones and complained. Teamsters cursed in whispers, as if the British might hear profanity across the Hudson. Horses, smelling panic, rolled white eyes at the crush on the road.

Behind them, on the far side of the river, Fort Washington had fallen.

The fact seemed too large for the night. Nearly three thousand Americans killed or captured, guns lost, stores gone, the British flag raised where confidence had stood only days before. Men repeated the number without believing it. Three thousand. Every repetition changed it from arithmetic into ghosts.

Paine had seen discouragement before. He had known it in English workshops, in excise offices, in the eyes of men whose petitions were received with smiles and buried under procedure. This was different. This discouragement had boots worn through at the soles. It coughed. It bled into dirty bandages. It asked whether liberty could survive until breakfast.

“Leave the tent,” an officer shouted.

Two soldiers had been wrestling a roll of canvas onto a cart already loaded past mercy.

“There won’t be another issued,” one protested.

“There won’t be a man to sleep in it if you stay. Leave it.”

The soldiers looked at the tent as poor men look at property they know they cannot replace. Then they let it fall.

Paine tightened his coat and went down toward the road. He carried a musket that still felt borrowed, though he had been carrying one for months. In Philadelphia, the name *Common Sense* could open a door, start an argument, fill a tavern table. Here it could not make a wagon lighter or conjure flour. Fame, he had learned, was almost useless in rain.

He had joined the Pennsylvania Associators in the summer, driven by what he called patriotism when others asked and by shame when he asked himself. It had become intolerable to urge ordinary men toward danger while remaining among inkpots. He had written that the sun never shone on a cause of greater worth. A sentence like that incurred a debt. He could not ask farmers, apprentices, dockworkers, and boys with down on their chins to pay it alone.

At first the men had treated him as a curiosity.

“You’re the fellow who abolished the king,” a broad-backed private had said, studying him across a campfire.

“On paper.”

“Aye. He appears not to have read it.”

The men laughed, and Paine laughed with them. The joke pleased him because it was true. George of Hanover still had fleets, regiments, foundries, credit, and hired German soldiers. Paine had clauses. The disparity might have humbled a different man. In Paine it produced a ferocious need to prove that clauses could acquire bayonets.

Soldiering gave him his first prolonged education in the distance between a cause and an army. The cause was natural right, self-government, the liberation of a continent from hereditary rule. The army was spoiled meat, wet powder, smoke in the eyes, quarrels over straw, and the lifelong mystery of where one had put one’s spoon.

Camp woke before light, usually because someone else had coughed through the night. Fires had to be nursed from embers. Water had to be found and then distrusted. Men scratched at lice without embarrassment. They spoke of home with elaborate indifference. They cursed Congress, praised General Washington, mocked officers, begged tobacco, misplaced flints, and carried rumors the way children carried sparks.

One rumor could cross a brigade faster than an order. The French had landed. The French would never land. New York had risen against the British. New York had invited them to dinner. General Howe was afraid to advance. General Howe was already behind them. Congress had voted every soldier a farm. Congress had forgotten the army existed.

Paine listened because listening had always been his truest apprenticeship. The language of soldiers was stripped of ceremony. A hungry man did not say the supply arrangements were inadequate. He said there was no bread. A frightened man did not discourse on strategic reverses. He asked whether the road behind them remained open.

The road from Fort Lee remained open only because they took it at once.

General Nathanael Greene, under whom Paine served for a time as an aide-de-camp, moved through the confusion with a face composed against bad news. Paine admired Greene’s mind. It had the practical geometry he himself lacked: roads, distances, forage, hours, numbers of men who could be expected to arrive as opposed to numbers promised. Greene had opposed abandoning Fort Washington until calamity settled the argument. Now he carried that error without theatrics, setting about the rescue of what remained.

Washington appeared near the column as a darker shape upon a dark horse. No engraving had yet frozen him into marble. He looked exhausted. His cloak was damp; his face held the loneliness of a man receiving ten opinions and permitted no uncertainty of his own. Men straightened as he passed, not because they thought him infallible but because his composure seemed one of the army’s remaining provisions.

Paine watched him and understood that symbols could be hungry too.

The column moved west through Bergen County. Dawn did not improve the scene. It revealed abandoned cookware, split shoes, faces gray from insufficient sleep. Frost whitened the fields. Families stood at gates while the army passed, some offering water, some staring, some already calculating which flag to welcome if Cornwallis arrived by noon.

At a bridge over the Hackensack, the press became dangerous. Wagons locked wheels. A horse went down between shafts and screamed until a teamster put a ball into its head. The report cracked over the water, and half the men turned as if the British had caught them.

“Forward,” an officer called. “Only a horse. Forward.”

Only a horse, Paine thought, stepping around the steam rising from its blood. War revised the meaning of *only*.

They crossed, dismantled what they could, and kept moving. Behind them came Cornwallis with disciplined troops and proper shoes. The British advance existed at first as distant cannon, then as reports from riders, then as a pressure in every conversation. Redcoats were said to be six miles away, then twelve, then three. No one knew. Everyone looked backward.

Rain arrived fine as needles. Paine’s paper, what little he carried, had to be kept beneath his coat. At halts he made notes on scraps, bracing them against his knee or the flat of a drum. Not polished thoughts. Fragments. Images. A phrase overheard. A curse. The shape a man’s bare heel left in frozen mud.

He had written *Common Sense* in the forward rush of possibility. Independence had then seemed not easy, exactly, but clean. Once declared, it would draw courage out of Americans the way sunlight drew shoots from soil. Britain would discover the expense of subduing a continent. Europe would perceive a new power. Reason would clarify interest.

Reason had not accounted for diarrhea.

It had not accounted for the smell inside a crowded barn after three hundred wet men slept there. It had not accounted for militia enlistments expiring in the middle of disaster, or for farmers hiding grain from both armies, or for the particular silence of a soldier reading a letter that told him his child had died while he was away defending posterity.

Yet Paine did not conclude that reason had failed. He concluded that reason, if it meant anything, must pass through mud.

At Newark the army paused long enough to become visible to itself. Units had shrunk. Some companies seemed no larger than families. Men whose terms were ending stood in knots, avoiding the looks of those who meant to continue. They had done what they had promised. Many had crops neglected, shops closed, wives trying to bargain for salt without coin. The republic had no right, Paine knew, to demand vows it could not feed.

Still, when he saw men step out of line and begin the road home, anger leapt before compassion could restrain it.

“You’ll go now?” he demanded of one Pennsylvania man he knew, a cooper named Amos Fry.

Fry’s cheeks hollowed under a week’s beard. One shoe was bound with rag.

“My time is served.”

“The enemy does not consult our enlistments.”

“My children don’t consult them either.”

Paine opened his mouth, equipped with a continent’s worth of argument.

Fry took a folded paper from his pocket. It was soft from being read. “My wife says the youngest is fevered. Says the landlord has taken the cow.”

The argument died unspoken.

“I have marched,” Fry said. “I have stood watch. I have eaten what they called beef. Don’t tell me I love liberty less because I love my own.”

Paine looked down at the rag around the man’s foot. “I won’t.”

Fry shifted the musket on his shoulder. “Write something to the men with whole shoes.”

Then he went.

The sentence followed Paine all the way to Brunswick.

There the Raritan lay cold under a low sky. Stores had to be removed or destroyed before the British reached them. Men hauled barrels until their hands opened. Paine helped with a crate of cartridges and felt his back seize. He was nearly forty, older than many of the officers shouting him forward, and the army had no use for the age of a man’s ideas.

At night he lay on a floor beside strangers and could not sleep. Someone whispered a prayer. Someone else muttered in fever. From outside came the occasional grind of a wagon leaving west.

Paine thought of the people who had purchased *Common Sense* in such numbers that printers could scarcely keep pace. Where were they now? He had imagined the public as a great body rising. He saw instead separate households making separate bargains with fear. New Jersey men accepting British protection papers. Merchants moving goods. Legislators fleeing. Men who had cheered independence in July now speaking of reconciliation as if they had merely misplaced it.

He felt betrayed, then ashamed of feeling betrayed. What had he expected—that courage, once summoned, stayed summoned? Courage was not a bell one rang. It was a fire that consumed its fuel.

Something had to feed it.

He rose in the dark, found his scraps, and wrote by the miserly flame of a tallow candle. The opening would not come. He tried grandeur and despised it. He tried accusation and heard only vanity. He wanted a sentence that would acknowledge terror without surrendering to it, a sentence broad enough to contain the man who stayed and the man who went home, severe enough to shame the comfortable, plain enough to be remembered by someone who could not read it for himself.

He wrote *These are difficult days*.

He crossed it out.

Difficulty was a poor man’s supper. It had no blood in it.

He wrote *This is the season in which the virtue of America must be known*.

Too stiff. A coat buttoned to the throat.

The candle guttered. He rubbed his eyes until color moved behind them. Around him slept the remnant of an army that had lost New York, lost forts, lost men by thousands, and was in danger of losing the story it told itself.

Times, he thought. Not a day. Not a battle. Times.

And what did such times do? They tested. They put courage under weight. They separated the metal that held from the metal that bent. They tried men as courts tried claims, as furnaces tried iron, as grief tried faith.

His pen moved.

*These are the times that try men’s souls.*

He sat back.

The sentence seemed less written than uncovered. It possessed a beat fit for marching and a gravity fit for burial. He said it aloud, softly. A sleeping man turned over. Rain tapped at the roof.

Paine wrote the next words with gathering certainty: *The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of their country; but he that stands it now, deserves the love and thanks of man and woman.*

There was Amos Fry in the phrase, though Paine knew the cooper had not shrunk. There were men of property who had shouted loud in safety and discovered moderation as the British approached. There was Paine himself, the pamphleteer compelled at last to test language beside a musket.

He did not spare the reader, but neither did he pretend liberty was cheap. *Tyranny, like hell, is not easily conquered.* That was the center. If freedom could be secured by a parade, it would be worth no more than bunting. What mattered cost. What mattered whether a people who had loved the sound of independence could endure its invoice.

By morning he had paragraphs, not yet a paper. He folded them into his coat and marched.

Princeton offered no rest. Then Trenton. Every river became a question: could the army cross before the British? Could boats be gathered? Could cannon be saved? Could men be made to move when their bodies had begun to refuse command?

The roads filled with civilians going the same direction. Carts held children, bedding, iron pots, clocks, cages of fowl. One old woman walked beside a wagon with a portrait pressed to her chest as if the painted ancestor required rescue more than food. A boy dragged a wooden horse on a string until one wheel broke; he stood in the road crying while soldiers parted around him.

Paine had written of a continent. The continent now presented itself as that child, furious over a broken toy no declaration could mend.

At Trenton, men searched the Delaware for boats while officers searched the men for obedience. The British were near enough to populate the opposite darkness. Washington’s army crossed into Pennsylvania under a sky the color of lead. Boats went back and returned, overloaded. Oars struck floating ice. Cannon had to be coaxed down slick banks, ferried, dragged up again. Men waited without fires lest the glow advertise them.

Paine crossed among the last movements of the retreat. Midstream, he looked back toward New Jersey. The shore was indistinct, a low black line. Somewhere beyond it British officers were writing reports that called the rebellion nearly finished.

He felt no sudden revelation, only fatigue and an anger so clean it warmed him.

“They think that river ends it,” said the soldier beside him.

“Then we must teach them geography,” Paine answered.

The soldier gave a tired laugh.

On the Pennsylvania bank, defeat acquired administrative habits. Units searched for quarters. Commissaries counted what they did not have. Officers estimated how many men would remain after the new year and found the answer unbearable. Congress, fearing Philadelphia would be taken, prepared to withdraw to Baltimore. Wealthy families loaded carriages. Loyalists allowed hope to show in their faces.

The city to which Paine returned was not the city that had rung bells for independence. Shops closed early. Metal workers hid useful stock. Rumor announced Howe every afternoon. There were plans to burn supplies rather than let the British take them. Paper money weakened. A sack of flour seemed to change price between breakfast and dusk.

In the streets Paine saw men recognize him and then look away, as if *Common Sense* had become an acquaintance who reminded them of an embarrassing enthusiasm.

He took a room, removed his shoes, and found blood dried into his stocking. For a while he sat on the edge of the bed unable to bend far enough to pull the cloth away. His hands shook from weariness.

This, he thought, was the grand theory of republican government: a man alone, trying to remove a dirty stocking without tearing his heel.

He began to laugh. It was not amusement. The laugh climbed toward hysteria and stopped only when he pressed both palms against his face.

Sleep took him and returned him to New Jersey. In the dream the retreating column stretched without end. Every man carried a printing press instead of a musket. The presses grew heavier, the road deeper, the British drums louder. When Paine woke, dawn had barely grayed the window, and the opening sentence waited intact in his mind.

He found Melchior Styner and Charles Cist, printers willing to put the new work into type while invasion hung over the city. The shop had the tense clutter of men trying to conduct business at the edge of calamity. Type lay sorted in cases. Damp sheets hung. Ink shone black under lamplight. The press itself, with its screw and bar, resembled an instrument of constructive torture.

“How long?” Paine asked.

Styner glanced over the pages. “If the militia does not take my men?”

“If.”

“If the British do not take Philadelphia?”

“If.”

“If paper can be had?”

“If.”

Styner looked at him. “Then as fast as hands allow.”

Paine worked at a table while compositors lifted letters one by one. He revised into the noise of the shop. Metal touched metal. The form filled. His prose expanded beyond the opening challenge into argument, memory, rebuke. He addressed panic not as a philosopher above it but as a man who had marched inside it.

He recalled the retreat honestly: officers and men harassed, exhausted, often without rest, covering, or provision, yet centered upon one wish—that the country would turn out and help drive the enemy back. He attacked the British claim to rule as he had before, but now the abstraction wore Cornwallis’s boots. He described the tavern keeper who had boasted that he wanted peace in his day even if trouble came in his child’s. Paine’s contempt sharpened. A father who purchased ease with his child’s bondage had mistaken cowardice for care.

He wrote against Howe’s offer of pardons. A pardon presumed guilt. America had committed no crime in refusing slavery. He turned the British general’s proclamation around like a captured gun and fired it back: if a thief entered a house, could he pardon the owner for resisting?

Hours passed. The city darkened. At times Paine’s head dropped toward his chest and jerked up again. Styner brought coffee that tasted of burnt roots. Cist argued over a word to fit the line. A compositor, no more than sixteen, set the famous opening without visible awe, reaching into compartments for each reversed letter.

That humbled Paine more than praise would have. The sentence was already leaving him. In type it belonged to hands he did not know.

He signed it *Common Sense*.

His given name had become less useful than the title of his first pamphlet. This did not trouble him as much as it might have. Thomas Paine had failed at trades, offices, marriages, and respectability. Common Sense had helped declare a nation. If the mask carried farther than the man, let the mask go first.

The pages came off the press in small numbers, then more. Copies moved by rider and wagon, folded into saddlebags, thrust into hands at taverns, ferries, and guard posts. No royalty delayed them. No concern for ownership. The object was circulation, as with blood.

Paine gave away what claim he might have made upon the proceeds. He had not written for profit, and he wanted no man to weigh patriotism against the price of a meal. Printers still needed paper and wages; copies were sold where they had to be. But the words escaped payment whenever opportunity allowed.

He imagined them traveling north along roads he had just walked south. The thought possessed him. A paragraph set in a Philadelphia shop could reach a man beside the Delaware, pass from a literate sergeant to a company gathered close, enter the ears of men who would never know the shape of Paine’s hand.

Reports returned before certainty did. A courier said the paper was being read in camp. Another had heard an officer reciting the beginning beside a fire, the soldiers answering the phrase *summer soldier* with bitter laughter. A Pennsylvania lieutenant who came into the city for supplies told Paine he had unfolded a copy before his men.

“All of it?” Paine asked.

“As much as they’d stand still for.”

“And what did they say?”

“One asked whether words could issue shoes.”

Paine winced.

The lieutenant smiled. “Then he asked me to read the first part again.”

Before the movement on Trenton, copies crossed among the troops on the western bank. Captains and sergeants read them aloud to men waiting in the cold, sometimes by firelight, sometimes in the dark to groups that could hear a voice but not see the page. No ceremony attended it. Wind stole some sentences. Men stamped their feet. One company heard only the opening and the line about tyranny before an order sent them down the road.

Paine was not among them. He was in Philadelphia, listening to bells, rumors, and the restless city, while his words went in his place.

He later built the scene from what soldiers told him: the sleet hardening on coats, the cannon wrapped to quiet their wheels, the dark descent toward McConkey’s Ferry. He pictured an officer holding a blurred sheet and calling over the weather, *These are the times that try men’s souls.* He pictured faces lifting, not transformed, not freed of fear, but recognized. The paper told them that the cold was real, the danger was real, the chance of defeat was real, and standing mattered anyway.

That was all prose could honestly do. It could not cross the river for them.

On Christmas night Washington’s force entered the boats. Ice struck the hulls. The storm came hard from the northeast, snow and rain mixing into a punishment. Men held muskets above wet feet. Horses shifted. Artillerymen cursed ropes and current. The passage took longer than planned, and dawn threatened the surprise.

Paine knew none of this as it happened. In Philadelphia he walked to the window at every sound in the street. He tried to write and failed. He lay down clothed and rose again. His mind returned to the black river he had crossed in retreat. Somewhere beyond the city, men were gambling the republic with frozen hands.

News of Trenton arrived piecemeal.

First came a rider with a claim too good to trust: the Hessians taken. Then numbers. Then contradiction. Then another messenger, mud to his thighs, grinning before he spoke. Washington had struck at dawn. Colonel Rall was mortally wounded. Hundreds of Hessian soldiers had surrendered. American losses were astonishingly light in battle, though men had frozen and fallen on the march.

The city’s mood did not brighten so much as ignite. Doors opened. Bells spoke. People who had whispered the day before found full voices. Strangers embraced and immediately began improving the story. The Hessian garrison doubled in every telling. Washington crossed through ice thick enough to hold a church. Men who had considered British protection discovered they had always predicted victory.

Paine stood amid the rejoicing and felt an emotion for which pride was too simple.

The paper had circulated. The men had crossed. Trenton had been won. It was tempting—deliciously tempting—to arrange these facts as cause and effect, with his sentence at the head of the column. Others did it for him. A printer clapped his shoulder and declared that *The Crisis* had saved the army. A tavern speaker shouted that Paine’s pen had defeated the Hessians.

He wanted to believe them.

Then he remembered the horse killed at the Hackensack, Amos Fry’s fevered child, the soldiers whose feet had marked blood on the road. He remembered that men, not metaphors, had dragged cannon through the storm.

“The pen did not load a single musket,” he said.

“It may have kept one man from setting his down,” the printer replied.

That answer stayed with him.

The victory at Princeton followed in early January, another swift blow that made British certainty stumble. Washington moved into winter quarters at Morristown. Enlistments were coaxed longer. Militia appeared in greater numbers. New Jersey, which had seemed ready to submit, became dangerous ground for British foraging parties. The revolution did not become safe. It became possible again.

Paine returned to his desk before triumph could soften him. The first number had been called *The American Crisis*, and a number implied another. He addressed the second to Lord Howe, answering paternal declarations from a British commander who offered mercy while bringing war. Paine would not permit empire to play the injured father. Britain and America, he argued, were political parties, not parent and child; the connection of birth conferred no eternal right of command.

He wrote with more confidence now, but not with less urgency. One victory could become a narcotic. Americans loved celebration and forgot supply. They praised soldiers in print and left them unpaid in fact. They rang bells while widows negotiated debts.

At camp again, he saw what victory looked like after the cheering ended. Morristown huts smoked badly. Smallpox moved with an efficiency generals envied. Men submitted to inoculation with dread, choosing a measured danger over the disease’s random slaughter. Wounds reopened in cold. Surgeons worked with tools that seemed better suited to carpentry. A pile of amputated cloth carried the shapes of missing limbs.

Paine visited a hospital and nearly fled from the smell.

Rows of men lay beneath blankets of incompatible colors. Some slept, some stared, some pleaded for water seconds after drinking it. A boy from Maryland gripped Paine’s wrist and mistook him for a brother.

“Tell Mother I wasn’t afeared,” the boy said.

Paine could not make his voice work.

“Tell her.”

“I will.”

The boy died before evening. Paine never learned the mother’s name.

That was war’s cost too: promises impossible to deliver.

He had argued for independence and did not repent it. British prison ships held Americans in rot and darkness. Raiding parties took food from households that had little. Hessian hire, imperial coercion, the burning of towns—none of it suggested that submission would have been merciful. But the righteousness of a cause did not disinfect its suffering. A republic could be born justly and still be born in screams.

At night Paine found himself thinking of England, of the mother he had left, of the quiet people who would read reports calling American dead rebels and British dead heroes. Governments distributed moral names with uniforms. The body beneath did not know the difference.

He began to distrust anyone who spoke lightly of sacrifice, himself included.

The later *Crisis* papers grew from that distrust. Whenever the cause sagged, he returned to it. He wrote through reversals, occupation, political quarrels, shortages, and years that wore novelty from revolution. The numbers would continue almost as long as the war, sometimes fierce, sometimes argumentative, sometimes swollen by his inability to leave a defeated point unkicked. He addressed generals, commissioners, English readers, American citizens. He mocked British power and scolded American fatigue. He tried again and again to turn circumstance into endurance.

The first paper remained different. Its ink contained the New Jersey road.

He could see that road whenever he repeated the opening. Historians might one day make the retreat an arrow on a map. They would print dates, troop strengths, rivers crossed. They might call Trenton a turning point and mean, correctly, that events changed direction there.

But Paine knew a turning point did not feel like a point to those inside it. It felt like wet socks. It felt like leaving a tent because there was no time to save it. It felt like a man going home to a sick child while another judged him. It felt like a printer setting type while wondering whether the press must be hidden tomorrow. It felt like fear enduring one hour longer than expected and discovering, with surprise, that it had become courage.

In the spring of 1777, as thaw turned roads to mud, Paine stood outside a Philadelphia print shop and watched a veteran approach on a wooden crutch. The man’s left trouser leg was pinned below the knee. He carried a copy of the first *Crisis*, folded small and dirty at the edges.

“You wrote this?” the veteran asked.

Paine had learned caution around praise. “I did.”

The man held it out. “Read the beginning.”

“You know the beginning.”

“I’d hear it.”

People passed around them. A cart rattled over stones. Paine took the paper and unfolded it. The sheet had been damp more than once. A brown stain crossed the margin.

He read the sentence.

The veteran nodded, not smiling. “That was true.”

Paine looked at the missing shape below the pinned cloth. The old appetite for applause deserted him.

“Was it worth it?” he asked.

The man considered with a seriousness that made Paine regret the question. At last he said, “It must be.”

Not *it was*. *It must be.*

There lay the burden the living placed upon the future. Victory would not justify the cost merely by existing. The nation had to become worthy of the men it had consumed. Independence was no receipt marked paid. It was an obligation renewed by every grave.

Paine folded the paper and returned it.

Soon Congress would offer him a different kind of service: a desk, official correspondence, access to the hidden machinery of alliance. He would exchange camp smoke for committee rooms and discover that danger did not always wear a red coat. Ambition, secrecy, money, and pride could injure a revolution as surely as cannon.

For that morning, though, he remained beside the veteran while Philadelphia moved around them. Neither spoke. The city smelled of horses, bread ovens, river damp, and the first softness of spring.

Somewhere a hammer struck metal in a steady rhythm.

Paine heard in it the march through New Jersey, the press coming down, the knock of ice against a boat he had not boarded. He heard the trial inside the times and knew it had not ended at Trenton.

The souls of men were still being tried.

So was his.
