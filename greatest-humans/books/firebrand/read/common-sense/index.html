Common Sense

A pamphlet that made independence speakable

Winter tightened around Philadelphia until every street seemed drawn in iron.

Ice edged the Delaware. Cart wheels struck frozen ruts with the sound of musket fire, and smoke from a hundred chimneys settled low over the roofs. In Thomas Paine’s room, the fire consumed money faster than it produced warmth. He wrote in his coat, rose to stamp feeling back into his feet, then bent over the table again while his breath faded above the page.

At the top of the first sheet he had written two words: *Plain Truth*.

Beneath them waited a wilderness of scratched sentences. He had begun three times with the king and three times torn the opening away. To attack George III at once would satisfy Paine but lose the reader who still toasted His Majesty while cursing His Majesty’s ministers. He needed to lead that reader to the conclusion, not shout it from the other side.

He dipped his pen and wrote: *Some writers have so confounded society with government—*

That was better. Begin before Britain. Begin before kings.

A knock interrupted him. Dr. Benjamin Rush entered with cold in his face and a packet of newspapers under his arm. He glanced at the neglected fire, added coal without asking, and held his hands above it.

“You mean to freeze in the service of liberty,” Rush said.

“It is economical. A dead author requires no paper.”

“A dead author meets no printer either.”

Paine cleared the only other chair of books. Rush sat and looked toward the pages.

“May I?”

“Not if you intend to remain my friend.”

“I intend to remain useful.”

Paine passed him several sheets. He moved to the window while Rush read. Outside, a woman pulled a child around a frozen puddle. A drayman shouted at a stalled horse. Ordinary life maintained its confidence even with armies in the field. The city bought bread, swept steps, delivered wood, and debated whether the political world was ending.

Behind him, paper rustled.

The news had grown worse through autumn. The king had refused even to receive the Olive Branch Petition as the plea of loyal subjects and had declared the colonies in open rebellion. British warships had burned Falmouth in Massachusetts. Reports said more troops would come, including hired soldiers from German states. In Virginia, Lord Dunmore had promised freedom to enslaved men belonging to rebels if they joined the British standard, making liberty both weapon and accusation. Every post brought another reason reconciliation was impossible, followed by another public assurance that reconciliation remained the object.

Rush put down the pages.

“You have compared monarchy to original sin.”

“Unfairly to sin, perhaps.”

“And you intend to print this?”

“That is the weakness in my plan.”

Rush ignored the joke. “Many men agree with a portion. Few will follow all the way.”

“Then they do not agree with the portion. They merely admire it.”

“You cannot drive a cautious man by insulting his caution.”

Paine turned from the window. “I know. That is why I begin with government. Let him build it with me. Let him see that society is produced by our wants and government by our wickedness. One is a blessing; the other, even in its best state, a necessary evil. Then ask whether the hereditary part of the British arrangement serves any need except its own continuance.”

Rush tapped the manuscript. “You ask it rather forcefully.”

“Force has already been introduced into the discussion. I did not march on Concord.”

A reluctant smile passed over the doctor’s face. He read the title aloud. “*Plain Truth*.”

“It says what it is.”

“It says what you think of it.”

“You have an improvement?”

Rush walked once across the cramped room. “The argument is that separation is not frenzy, treason, or the dream of a faction. It is merely—”

He stopped.

“Common sense,” he said.

The phrase entered the room without ceremony.

Paine repeated it. Common sense. A faculty belonging to no rank, requiring no university, owned as completely by a blacksmith as by a baronet. It did not announce revelation from above. It appealed to judgment already present.

He took up his pen and crossed out the title.

“That,” he said, writing the new words, “will travel.”

For the next weeks, he lived inside the pamphlet.

Morning found him revising what night had produced. Afternoon carried him through print shops, coffeehouses, and rooms where political men spoke cautiously behind closed doors. Evening returned him to the table. Some nights Rush read pages; on others, a trusted acquaintance listened while Paine watched the man’s face more closely than he heard his own prose. Confusion showed first around the eyes. Agreement appeared as stillness.

He wanted no sentence to depend upon the reader’s schooling. Lawyers dressed weak ideas in Latin because Latin prevented the injured party from seeing the weakness. Paine preferred short Saxon words that struck the mind whole. He cut qualifications, then restored those honesty required. He replaced abstractions with objects a reader could hold: a house, an island, a continent, a child, a thief breaking into a home.

The magazine had taught him rhythm. An argument could not be one long advance. It required places for the reader to stand, look back, and recognize how far he had come. Paine built those places with questions.

Why did a small island rule a vast continent?

Why should a country be governed forever by a power three thousand miles away?

Why should one man, selected only by birth, possess authority over millions yet unborn?

If Britain was America’s parent, as loyalists liked to say, what parent made war upon its children?

The answers seemed obvious once the questions had been stripped of inherited awe. That was the work: not inventing truth, but removing the costumes in which power hid.

Monarchy gave him the greatest pleasure and the greatest danger. American opposition had long protected the king by blaming Parliament and corrupt ministers. The distinction allowed resistance to appear loyal. Paine meant to destroy it.

He read the Bible not as a churchman seeking comfort but as an advocate preparing cross-examination. Many Americans who would ignore a philosopher listened when Scripture spoke. He returned to the Israelites demanding a king and to Samuel’s warning that a king would take their sons, daughters, fields, vineyards, servants, and flocks. Monarchy’s sacred origin proved, on inspection, to be a rejection of divine government and a sentence of punishment.

One evening Rush found the Bible open among histories of England.

“Your father would recognize this method,” Rush said.

The mention of Joseph Paine made Thomas pause. His father’s Quaker faith distrusted violence and human vanity; it acknowledged no natural nobility in one soul above another. Yet many Philadelphia Friends feared separation because separation meant war, and war violated the testimony of peace.

“My father might recognize the question,” Paine said. “I doubt he would approve the answer.”

“Does that trouble you?”

“If it did not, I would be a poorer son.”

He bent over the manuscript again. Hereditary succession was monarchy’s central absurdity. Even if one man happened to be wise, birth could not guarantee wisdom in his heir. Nature herself mocked the proposition by so frequently giving mankind an ass for a lion. Paine smiled when he wrote the line, then argued with himself for an hour over whether the ridicule weakened the case. In the end he kept it. Reverence could survive logic. Laughter was more corrosive.

But he refused to make the pamphlet merely an attack. Destruction without a design frightened sensible people. He sketched a government arising from the colonies themselves: frequent elections, broad representation, a continental congress, a charter securing freedom and property. The details mattered less than the principle. Authority could flow upward from the people instead of downward from a crown.

Then came the practical case. America possessed timber, iron, hemp, and shipyards. It could build a navy over time. Its trade wanted every harbor in Europe, not dependence upon British permission. Remaining connected to Britain dragged the colonies into Britain’s enemies and Britain’s wars. The continent’s size and distance made permanent rule from London ridiculous. The time to form a government was now, while the population was sufficient and the occasion united the colonies.

Paine had heard men say independence must wait. They meant to sound prudent, but delay was not neutral. Every year increased the Crown’s opportunities to divide the colonies. Every battle created new injuries. Every plea after bloodshed trained Britain to expect submission.

“Time does not negotiate,” he told a skeptical merchant at the London Coffee House. “It only charges interest.”

The merchant stirred his coffee. “And if France seizes us the moment Britain leaves?”

“France has no greater love of Britain’s empire than we do. Independent, we may trade and make alliances. Dependent, we inherit every British quarrel.”

“You make nations sound like shopkeepers.”

“Shopkeepers generally understand interest better than kings.”

The men at the next table had stopped pretending not to listen.

Paine tested images that way. He took an argument among ordinary talk and watched whether it could stand without scaffolding. If a listener repeated a phrase ten minutes later, Paine carried it home. If the point required three explanations, he rebuilt it. A pamphlet addressed to the inhabitants of America could not remain a private performance between men who owned libraries.

At times exhaustion gave him false certainty. He would fill pages after midnight and wake convinced he had captured the entire case, only to discover in daylight that the sentences marched beautifully toward nowhere. Then he swore, lit the fire, and began cutting.

The cold deepened. Ink thickened in its pot. His fingers cramped around the pen. He slept in pieces and dreamed of the *London Packet*, except that the ship was made of paper and every wave bore the king’s face. On waking he sometimes felt the same feverish pressure that had accompanied his crossing. His body remembered how close it had come to dying before America knew his name.

That memory imposed urgency. He had not been spared for caution.

By late December the manuscript had the shape he wanted. He read the whole aloud in his room. The argument began with first principles, passed through the fraud of monarchy, confronted the colonial crisis, and ended with America’s ability to stand alone. It did not ask the reader to hate England. Paine took care over that distinction. England was the home of his birth and language, full of people no freer in choosing ministers than the colonists were. Europe, not Britain alone, was America’s parent. The quarrel was with political connection and royal power.

He laid down the final sheet. Silence filled the room.

On paper, independence looked not wild but overdue.

Getting it printed was another matter.

A printer was not a neutral machine. He owned the type, paper, press, and name authorities could seize. Prosecution for sedition was no abstraction to men whose livelihood stood behind a shop window. Several who read or heard of the manuscript admired it and found reasons not to touch it. The political temperature was high, the Congress had not declared for separation, respectable leaders still denied intending it, and a pamphlet attacking the king might turn its printer into a public target.

Rush proposed Robert Bell.

Bell was another Scots immigrant, a bookseller and auctioneer as well as a printer, with theatrical habits and a taste for ventures safer men rejected. He sold books by turning commerce into performance. His advertisements spoke loudly. His rooms seemed arranged not for quiet literary judgment but for collision.

“He is bold,” Rush said.

“Is he sound?”

“Boldness may be the sounder quality at present.”

They found Bell among books, bills, and tied bundles of paper. He read portions standing up, his lips moving. His eyes sharpened as the argument advanced. Paine could almost hear the calculations behind them: risk, demand, notoriety, paper, price.

“Written by an Englishman,” Bell said, considering the proposed title page.

“The name is not to appear.”

“Names sell books.”

“This name might sell the printer’s furniture at government auction.”

Bell looked offended by the suggestion that fear guided him. “My furniture is accustomed to danger.”

Paine explained that his share of any profit should serve the public cause rather than himself. He had heard how badly the soldiers in the northern campaign needed winter clothing; his portion could buy mittens for the men who had followed General Montgomery toward Quebec. Bell’s share would remain Bell’s. Their understanding about costs and profits was still less exact than it should have been, but urgency made precision seem small.

“A thousand for the first impression,” Bell said.

Paine looked at the pages.

A thousand readers. Perhaps fewer, with copies shared; perhaps many more, with the pamphlet read aloud. Still, against a continent the number seemed fragile.

“A thousand to begin,” he said.

The manuscript entered the printing room.

There Paine ceased to own its pace. Compositors stood at type cases beneath winter light, selecting letters one by one and setting his sentences backward in metal. The first page became a locked form. An inked proof emerged, transforming his familiar handwriting into the impersonal authority of print.

He examined every line. An omitted *not* could reverse a principle. A broken letter could make a strong sentence look diseased. He changed words at the edge of the press, to Bell’s annoyance, and returned corrected proofs dark with marks.

“You gave me a finished manuscript,” Bell said.

“I gave you a manuscript I had finished writing. Printing has exposed its defects.”

“Printing exposes costs.”

“Correct this line.”

The pressmen worked. Ink balls struck the type with soft, wet beats. Paper descended, the frisket folded, the bed rolled beneath the platen. The bar came across; the screw pressed; the bed rolled out. A boy lifted the printed sheet and hung it to dry. Again. Ink, paper, pressure. Again.

Paine watched his private winter multiply.

On the title page, beneath *COMMON SENSE*, appeared the declaration that it was addressed to the inhabitants of America. There was no author’s name, only “Written by an Englishman.” Lines from James Thomson’s *Liberty* promised that the subject was not the cause of one country alone but, in time, of all mankind.

The claim was immense. Paine meant it.

On January 10, 1776, the pamphlet went on sale.

At first nothing visible happened. A customer entered Bell’s shop and bought one with another book. A messenger took a bundle. Copies went to coffeehouses and booksellers. Paine walked outside into a bitter morning and found Philadelphia exactly where it had been. Bells rang the hour. A butcher swept dirty snow from his threshold. Two sailors quarreled over a cart.

He had expected no thunder, he told himself.

By afternoon Bell had sent for more copies from the bindery. The pamphlet was issued in stitched gatherings, quick and cheap, meant to move from hand to hand. Buyers came asking for it by title. The words were easy to remember. Common sense: everyone wished to possess it, and everyone suspected his opponent did not.

The first thousand disappeared with astonishing speed.

Paine began to hear the pamphlet before he saw its effects. In a tavern near the river, a man stood by the hearth reading to a room of sailors, mechanics, and laborers. His finger traveled beneath the lines. He was not a graceful reader; he halted over long words and restarted sentences when the room grew loud. No one mocked him.

“Read the part about the island,” somebody called.

The man found the place. “Small islands, not capable of protecting themselves, are the proper objects for kingdoms to take under their care; but there is something very absurd in supposing a continent to be perpetually governed by an island.”

A ship carpenter struck his palm on the table. “There. There it is.”

He said it not as a man receiving a new thought but as one recognizing his own thought at last.

Paine stood near the back with his hat low. Heat rose into his face. He had wanted the argument to escape him. He had not anticipated the strange intimacy of hearing another man lend it breath.

“Who wrote it?” a sailor asked.

“An Englishman.”

“Then England has at least one useful export.”

Laughter filled the room. Paine laughed too, anonymous among his readers.

Copies traveled north with post riders and south in saddlebags. Booksellers ordered them by the dozen, then by the hundred. Newspapers printed extracts. Local presses began issuing their own editions in Salem, Newburyport, Newport, Providence, Hartford, Norwich, Lancaster, Albany, New York, Baltimore, and beyond. Paine did not attempt to preserve a monopoly. Copyright, where it existed at all, seemed absurd beside the object. He wanted reproduction.

The pamphlet crossed social boundaries unevenly but relentlessly. Merchants debated its claims over ledgers. Artisans pooled coins for a copy. Soldiers heard it around campfires. In farmhouses, one literate member of a family read while the others worked or listened. Women discussed its case in parlors, kitchens, shops, and correspondence, though the government it imagined still assumed political power in male hands. Free Black Philadelphians heard white colonists speak more boldly of natural liberty while knowing how partial that liberty remained. Enslaved people did not need a pamphlet to understand the difference between freedom and its counterfeit.

Not every reader was persuaded. Some were appalled.

A prosperous loyalist confronted Paine without knowing him at a coffeehouse. The man held a copy rolled tightly in one fist.

“This is poison,” he announced to the room. “The author reasons from government’s abuses to the destruction of government.”

“Does he?” Paine asked.

“Plainly. He would set a mob above law.”

“I thought he proposed representative government.”

“Government without a king is no government.”

“The Dutch may be surprised to learn it.”

The loyalist narrowed his eyes. “You defend the fellow warmly.”

“I dislike seeing a man condemned for arguments his critic has not read.”

“I have read every word.”

“Then you have misunderstood him with unusual diligence.”

A few men coughed to hide amusement. The loyalist’s face darkened. Paine felt the familiar pleasure of combat, then checked it. Humiliating one opponent might harden five listeners. He made himself ask the man what protection the Crown now supplied that could not be supplied by Americans themselves. The exchange continued, fierce but substantive. When the loyalist left, he took the pamphlet with him.

That too was circulation.

The political reaction traveled through private letters before it appeared in resolutions. George Washington, commanding the army outside Boston, wrote that *Common Sense* was working a powerful change in the minds of many men. Paine heard the phrase repeated by someone who had seen a copy of the letter. It affected him more than praise from any drawing room. Soldiers were enduring a winter siege under short enlistments, scarce powder, poor clothing, and uncertain purpose. If the pamphlet clarified what they suffered for, it had entered history beyond print.

Delegates in Congress read it while publicly maintaining careful positions. Some welcomed the shift it created but would not yet endorse the conclusion. Others thought it dangerously democratic, contemptuous of the mixed constitution, and reckless about the difficulty of forming a union. Men who had spent years constructing constitutional arguments resented an unknown Englishman reducing the matter to images repeated in taverns.

The resentment did not slow sales.

Anonymity frayed. Rush knew. Bell knew. Trusted friends knew, and every trusted friend possessed another trusted friend. Paine’s habits betrayed him as well. He could not hear a mistaken account of the author’s meaning without correcting it too precisely.

At Bell’s shop, a customer praised the passage comparing the royal government to a housebreaker.

“Though the Englishman errs in saying April is the best month to declare,” the customer added.

“He does not say April,” Paine replied. “He argues that winter permits time for deliberation before the campaign.”

The customer studied him.

Bell, behind the counter, became suddenly interested in an invoice.

“You know his argument closely,” the man said.

“I know what I read.”

Outside, Bell caught Paine by the sleeve. “You’ll announce yourself to the city one correction at a time.”

“Then the city should learn to read accurately.”

The secret became one of those facts everyone denied knowing while repeating. For a little longer the title page protected the argument from biography. Readers could not dismiss it as the grievance of a failed exciseman, a recent immigrant, a man without property or colonial ancestry. The voice belonged to an Englishman and therefore, paradoxically, to anyone.

Success brought a quarrel with Bell.

The printer reported that expenses had consumed the first edition’s proceeds. Paper, setting, presswork, stitching, advertising: every item had a price, and Bell’s accounting left no profit to donate. Paine, seeing copies vanish and demand increase, suspected that numbers were being arranged to suit the man holding the ledger.

They argued in the shop while compositors tried not to listen.

“A thousand sold,” Paine said. “You cannot turn abundance into nothing by writing columns.”

“And you cannot turn costs into charity by refusing to understand them.”

“Show me the full account.”

“You imply dishonesty.”

“I request arithmetic.”

Bell slapped a hand upon the counter. “Your alterations delayed the press. Your paper required more sheets than first discussed. Copies were allowed for promotion. Dealers receive their portion.”

“And the army receives air.”

Each man believed himself injured. Bell had accepted the political risk when other printers hesitated. Paine had supplied the work and renounced his own reward. Neither had stated their bargain with sufficient care. Principle, pride, and money made a combustible ink.

Paine took the enlarged text to William and Thomas Bradford.

The Bradfords belonged to one of the colonies’ most established printing families. Their press gave him another route, and demand made hesitation less dangerous than it had been weeks before. Paine added material, corrected errors, and included an appendix that drove the argument toward immediate action.

Bell, unwilling to surrender a sensation, announced another edition of his own. The rival advertisements appeared like cannon fire. Bell proclaimed. The Bradfords replied. Each edition made readers aware that another might sell out. Their dispute, intended to divide the pamphlet’s profits, multiplied its audience instead.

Philadelphia’s presses labored. Apprentices carried sheets. Compositors redistributed worn type into cases only to set the same arguments again. Rag paper absorbed ink and passed into bundles. The pamphlet’s price fell in some editions, broadening its reach. Printers elsewhere copied from copies, introducing small changes, errors, and local advertisements. *Common Sense* ceased to be a single object. It became a swarm.

Paine worked on the appendix while public opposition gathered.

On January 20, a testimony associated with prominent Pennsylvania Quakers warned members against joining armed rebellion and condemned writings tending toward separation. The statement did not name him, but readers supplied the connection. It angered Paine partly because he understood the conscience behind it. His father’s silence stood again beside his chair.

He respected refusal to kill. He did not respect using religious authority to settle a civil argument for everyone else.

Rush urged care. “If you attack the Friends, Philadelphia will hear not your distinction but your contempt.”

“I do not attack their faith.”

“They may not separate faith from testimony.”

“Then I must.”

In the appendix he argued that religious bodies should not impose political conclusions and that mingling religion with government endangered both. Yet he also reached toward a larger vision. Freedom had been hunted around the globe; Asia and Africa had expelled her, Europe regarded her as a stranger, and England had warned her to depart. America could receive the fugitive and prepare an asylum for mankind.

He paused over that claim.

An asylum for mankind, in colonies where human beings were bought and sold.

The contradiction remained. It did not make freedom false; it made the present practice intolerable. Paine could not resolve in one pamphlet what commerce, law, race, and power had built over generations. But he refused to limit the ideal to what Americans currently deserved. A principle was useful precisely because it judged its proclaimers.

The enlarged edition carried a new urgency. He declared that the sun had never shone on a cause of greater worth. The cause belonged not to one town, colony, or generation. The continent must choose between forming its own government and allowing events, armies, and distant ministers to form its future.

By February, independence was no longer a word used only in whispers.

It appeared in debates, sermons, letters, committee rooms, and tavern disputes. Men rejected it loudly enough to make clear that it had entered the room. Colonial assemblies reconsidered old instructions binding their delegates against separation. Newspapers that had avoided the question printed arguments on both sides. The middle ground did not vanish, but it narrowed under every report of British preparations.

Paine traveled only a few streets and heard the change everywhere.

At a bookseller’s, a woman buying almanacs asked whether a new shipment of *Common Sense* had arrived. At a market stall, two farmers argued over whether the colonies could pay for a navy. A boy used the phrase “a continent ruled by an island” in mocking another boy’s command over a sled. Language had escaped into play.

The last incident delighted Paine most.

For a time the knowledge of authorship remained a half-secret, widening through Philadelphia in private. By the end of March, denial had become theater and his name entered public connection with the pamphlet. Fame came behind the language, awkward and intrusive. Strangers began to identify him. Some wanted to shake his hand. Some wished to inspect the man who had spoken with such authority and found his plain coat disappointing. Dinner invitations arrived from men who would not have noticed him a year earlier. They praised his genius, then advised him to soften his next piece.

He understood the transaction. Society welcomed dangerous ideas after they proved popular and attempted to domesticate the author with supper.

At one dinner a lawyer raised a glass. “To Mr. Paine, who has given America a vocabulary.”

Paine raised his own. “America supplied the meaning.”

Later the lawyer drew him aside. “You must take care now.”

“Of what?”

“Your influence.”

“Influence is only useful when spent.”

“It can also be lost.”

Paine looked across the room at silver reflecting candlelight. “Then it is not property.”

He was pleased by admiration despite distrusting it. He had spent years ignored by officials, dismissed by employers, and underestimated by men with better coats. To watch those same species of men seek him out produced a warmth no stove had managed. He knew vanity was a trap and stepped into it with one foot, promising himself the other remained free.

Critics gave him less dangerous instruction.

Some loyalists answered that Britain’s protection had enabled colonial prosperity, that independence would expose rival colonies to one another, that trade would collapse, that France or Spain would conquer what Britain released. James Chalmers, a Maryland planter and loyalist writing as “Candidus,” prepared a reply pointedly titled *Plain Truth*. He accused Paine of deception and his readers of being hurried toward ruin by seductive simplicity.

Paine read hostile arguments carefully. Bad objections bored him; strong ones improved the case. The colonies did possess conflicting interests. Union would not arise merely because he capitalized Providence. Governments could fail without kings. Majorities could oppress. War for independence might be long, expensive, and bloody.

None of those truths made distant hereditary rule legitimate.

John Adams admired the pamphlet’s effect while distrusting portions of its plan and style. Paine heard that the Massachusetts lawyer considered the proposed form too simple and the attack on the English constitution too sweeping. Adams possessed a constitutional mind, full of balances, branches, checks, and historical examples. Paine respected the machinery but suspected that men who loved machinery sometimes forgot who must operate it.

“He thinks I have not designed a government,” Paine told Rush.

“Have you?”

“I have argued for the right to design one. That must come first.”

“And afterward?”

“Afterward we argue about the furniture.”

Rush laughed. “You will discover that men kill over furniture.”

The political storm intensified as winter weakened. News from the south told of Norfolk burned on the first day of the year, first under British cannon and then through further destruction amid Patriot orders and confusion. The details were disputed; the ruins were not. In the north, Washington’s army waited. British ships gathered. Congress sought gunpowder, money, alliances, and a purpose it had not yet named.

Paine felt the pamphlet pulled into uses he could not control. Radicals cited it as proof the public was ready. Moderates used its popularity to warn that Congress must move carefully. Loyalists displayed its most insulting lines as evidence that resistance meant anarchy. Printers profited. Preachers condemned or borrowed its cadences. Men who had never met Paine invoked what the author surely intended.

He learned that publication was a form of surrender. The writer commanded only until the sheet left the press. After that, readers made the words part of their own fear, courage, interest, and memory.

One wet evening near the end of March, he returned to the tavern where he had first heard the pamphlet read aloud. The last snow along the gutters had collapsed into gray ridges. Inside, damp coats steamed near the fire.

Mr. Haskins, the Sussex farmer who had crossed the Atlantic with him, sat at a table.

For a moment Paine saw the ship again: low beams, foul water, fever, a lantern moving while the world fell. Haskins looked older but solid. He had found work with his cousin outside the city and come in with produce.

“Mr. Paine,” he said, rising. “Or must I call you the Englishman now?”

“Not unless I owe you money.”

Haskins gripped his hand. “I knew the voice before the name came out.”

“Did you agree with it?”

“My cousin did. I didn’t.”

Paine sat opposite him. “And now?”

Haskins turned his cup between his hands. “Now I don’t know. That is more than I allowed before.”

It was not conversion. It was better: movement honestly measured.

“What holds you?” Paine asked.

“War. My boys. If we declare, the king sends everything.”

“He may send everything whether you declare or not.”

“May. There’s the word. You write as if courage removes chance.”

Paine looked at the farmer’s rough hands and remembered those hands shaking with fever. “No. Courage begins when chance remains.”

Haskins considered that. “You always did have a sentence ready.”

“Only after wasting several.”

They spoke for an hour. Paine did not press. The pamphlet had already said what he could say in public. Here was the private cost: a father imagining sons beneath British bayonets, a tenant who had crossed for land and found a continent asking for allegiance. Independence was clear in principle and terrifying in application. Any writer who ignored the terror lied.

When Paine left, rain had begun. It tapped the brim of his hat and softened the last ice. Light shone from print shops where work continued after dark.

He stopped outside Bell’s. Through the window he saw a pressman draw the bar. The machine bowed and rose. A boy lifted a sheet. Paine could not tell whether they were printing his pamphlet, an answer to it, a newspaper, or a sermon. The distinction seemed less important than it once had. All were entering the same argument.

Less than fifteen months had passed since he had been carried ashore unable to stand. He had arrived with Franklin’s letter as his sole credential and America as a rumor. Now his words moved where he had never been. They entered army tents and assembly chambers. They crossed mountains in saddlebags. They were read beside fires by people whose names he would never know.

The scale of it frightened him.

He had not created the movement toward independence. Lexington had written in blood before he wrote in ink. Parliament, the king, colonial committees, soldiers, merchants, farmers, enslaved people seeking their own freedom, families mourning the dead—all had made the crisis. Paine had done something narrower and perhaps decisive. He had given scattered facts a shape. He had taken a conclusion guarded by educated men and returned it to the public in language that implied they had owned it all along.

Common sense did not belong to Thomas Paine.

That was why the title had worked.

At his lodging he climbed the stairs and found three letters pushed beneath the door. One praised him extravagantly. One accused him of inviting murder. The third asked permission to reprint the pamphlet in another colony.

He set the first two aside and answered the third.

No fee. No restriction. Print it.

Below, somewhere in the sleeping city, a press struck paper. Then struck again.

Independence was not yet declared. No new nation existed. Congress still approached the word with caution, and armies still fought beneath banners proclaiming loyalty they no longer felt. But the forbidden idea had become speakable. It had a title, a price, a place in coat pockets. A farmer could disagree with it by the fire. A soldier could carry it beside cartridges. A delegate could deny its influence while measuring the public it had changed.

Paine opened his window. Rain swept cold into the room, carrying the smells of coal smoke, horses, wet wood, and the river. Philadelphia murmured in darkness.

He thought of the old title, *Plain Truth*, crossed out beneath Rush’s suggestion. Truth was never plain while power profited from confusion. It had to be pulled from custom, cleaned of reverence, set in type, and carried hand to hand. Only then did it appear obvious.

On his desk lay a fresh sheet of paper.

The pamphlet had escaped him, and the storm it summoned had only begun.
