# Chapter 3: Atlantic Crossing

The last thing Thomas Paine saw clearly of England was a wall of wet brick slipping behind a veil of rain.

He stood at the rail of the *London Packet* with one hand clamped over his hat and the other inside his coat, where Benjamin Franklin’s letter crackled against his chest. The vessel strained down the Thames under a low October sky, nosing past crowded wharves, blackened warehouses, and ships whose masts made a leafless forest above the river. Bargemen shouted. Gulls wheeled in the smoke. London receded by degrees, unwilling to release him all at once.

Somewhere behind that damp horizon were the rooms in which his second marriage had failed, the excise office from which he had twice been dismissed, the creditors who had divided the remains of his little shop, and the men who had praised his petition for the excisemen and then returned to their dinners. He was thirty-seven years old. His possessions fit in a sea chest. His reputation, such as it was, would not have filled a pocket.

Yet inside his coat was a new beginning written in Franklin’s compact hand.

Paine touched the letter again, less to assure himself of its safety than to feel the fact of it. Franklin had called him an ingenious, worthy young man and commended him to his son-in-law, Richard Bache, in Philadelphia. That sentence seemed more substantial than the deck under Paine’s boots. Franklin, printer and philosopher, a man who had caught lightning and made kings’ ministers answer him, had seen something worth sending across an ocean.

“You’ll wear a hole through your waistcoat,” said a voice behind him.

Paine turned. A broad commercial traveler named Mr. Lewis stood with his feet planted apart, already practiced in the motion of the deck. He had spent the morning announcing that he had crossed twice before and had never once been sick.

“Then I shall arrive in America with ventilation,” Paine said.

Lewis laughed. “If we arrive. October is a poor month to bargain with the Atlantic.”

“I have made poorer bargains.”

The river widened. The packet gathered the wind, and the city’s sounds thinned until only rigging, canvas, and water remained. Paine kept his face toward England long after the shore had blurred into nothing. He waited for grief, but what came first was relief, so sharp that it embarrassed him. He had loved parts of the country fiercely: Thetford’s broad Norfolk sky, the chalk dust of his father’s staymaking shop, the arguments in London taverns, the stubborn decency of working men who measured every loaf against a week’s wages. But England had become a house in which every room was occupied by an old defeat.

When he finally turned west, the sea lay ahead like an unwritten page.

By the time they passed the Downs, the page had begun to pitch.

The first gale came out of the southwest and struck the packet broadside. Crockery broke below. A chicken escaped its coop and flapped through the steerage passage while sailors, indifferent to both bird and passengers, shortened sail in stinging rain. Paine discovered that political courage had no authority over the stomach. He spent a day and night on his narrow berth, rolling against the boards, promising several varieties of reform to a Creator who appeared intent on shaking every conviction out of him.

Mr. Lewis was sick before noon.

“Never once?” Paine managed when the man lurched past.

Lewis raised a hand in surrender and vanished toward the ladder.

The storm passed, but the motion did not. The ship breathed and complained around them. Timbers groaned in long, animal tones; ropes creaked; water slapped the hull inches from Paine’s head. In calm weather the sails hung loose and the packet wallowed in gray swells. In hard weather every object had to be wedged, lashed, or held. Salt worked into clothes and bedding. The air below decks thickened with bilge, unwashed bodies, lamp smoke, spoiled food, damp wool, and fear.

There were merchants in the cabin and laborers in steerage, two families seeking land, an apprentice going to an uncle in New Jersey, and a young woman who guarded a bandbox as though it contained the Crown Jewels. Their rank on shore determined the space allotted to them at sea, but the Atlantic was an energetic republican. It sent the gentleman’s basin skating through the same vomit as the carpenter’s cup.

Paine began to know the others by their arguments. Men revealed themselves quickly when confined. One blamed every contrary wind on the captain. Another saw papist designs in cloud formations. A farmer from Sussex, Mr. Haskins, had sold his tenancy after a rent increase and meant to join a cousin outside Philadelphia.

“They say a man may have fifty acres there and no squire riding across it,” Haskins said one evening.

“A squire can cross an ocean as readily as a farmer,” Paine replied.

“Aye, but he must pay his passage.”

That earned a grin. They sat beneath a smoking lantern while the packet climbed and fell, climbed and fell. Paine listened to stories of wages, rents, grain, taxes, and sons impressed into naval service. He had heard versions of them in every English county. Governments wrote their histories in wars and treaties, but ordinary people counted history in the price of bread and the distance between one pay day and the next.

When conversation turned to America, certainty became rumor. Boston had been punished for its tea. Parliament had closed its port. Troops occupied its streets. The colonies had sent men to a congress in Philadelphia, where they were said to have spoken of rights with astonishing boldness. No one knew whether bold speech would end in accommodation, arrests, or war.

“They are Englishmen,” Lewis insisted, restored enough to lecture. “They want the rights of Englishmen.”

“Which rights?” Paine asked.

“All of them.”

“Name three.”

Lewis frowned. “Representation. Property. Trial before a jury.”

“Good. And if Parliament says those rights are gifts which Parliament may withdraw?”

“Then Parliament is wrong.”

“That answer is more dangerous than you make it sound.”

The cabin quieted. Beyond the hull, black water rushed westward.

Paine had not boarded as a revolutionary. He had boarded as a ruined Englishman carrying a letter. But questions traveled more comfortably than people. They required no berth, consumed no biscuit, and multiplied in bad weather. If a right could be withdrawn at another man’s pleasure, was it a right? If millions might be bound by a body in which they had no voice, what transformed force into law? Custom? Distance? A royal seal?

He had challenged the government before, but always as a petitioner. The excisemen’s case had asked power to become more reasonable. The plea had been carefully made, printed, circulated, and ignored. On the Atlantic, with England behind him and no country yet beneath his feet, another possibility suggested itself: perhaps power’s refusal to listen was not an occasional defect. Perhaps it was the natural habit of power answerable only to itself.

The ship’s water began to stink.

At first there were jokes. Men held their noses over the casks and mixed their allowance with rum when they had it. The sailors said water always turned foul at sea and would turn sweet again. Then a child developed a fever. A deckhand followed. The young woman with the bandbox stopped appearing at meals, and the passage outside her berth carried the sour odor of sickness.

The captain ordered vinegar sprinkled below and bedding brought up whenever weather permitted. It did little. The fever moved invisibly from berth to berth. Faces flushed, then grayed. Teeth chattered under layers of blankets. The packet became a floating sickroom, its people counting each cough and wondering whether it would be their own next.

Paine helped where he could. He carried cups, emptied basins, and sat with Haskins through one long night while the farmer muttered about a field in Sussex and tried to climb from his berth to harvest it. Paine had no medical training beyond what a staymaker, exciseman, and perpetual reader might collect, but he knew that a cool cloth gave comfort and that the frightened should not be left alone.

“You’ll take it,” Mr. Lewis warned.

“Then I shall already know the accommodations.”

“That is not sense.”

“No. It is company.”

Three days later Paine woke with iron bands around his skull.

He tried to sit, and the berth swung away from him. Light from the passage stabbed his eyes. His skin burned while something deep inside him shook with cold. He called for water, drank, and retched until his ribs seized. After that, time broke apart.

There were intervals of unbearable clarity. He saw pitch bubbling between deck planks under a white sun. He saw a sailor’s cracked thumbnail against the rim of a cup. He heard the captain say that five were dead, though Paine could not tell whether the number belonged to this ship or some vessel in a dream. Then came distances without measure. The ceiling pressed down like a coffin lid. Voices stretched into chants. The packet seemed no longer to cross the sea but to fall endlessly through it.

In fever he returned to Thetford.

His father sat at the workbench, pulling whalebone through cloth with hands made heavy by labor. Joseph Paine had been a Quaker, quiet in speech, suspicious of ornament. In the dream he held up a finished stay, its rigid channels designed to force the human body into a shape fashion approved.

Who made the pattern? Thomas asked him.

His father did not answer.

Who decided that pain was proper merely because it was inherited?

The needle moved in and out. The room filled with seawater.

He saw Elizabeth, his first wife, pale as she had been before death took her and their child. He saw Elizabeth Ollive standing behind the counter of their failed shop, exhausted by promises. He saw tax collectors and smugglers, ministers and magistrates, every person he had disappointed and every office that had disappointed him. Their faces dissolved into one another until Franklin appeared among them, holding out a folded letter that the water would not wet.

Wake up, Franklin said.

Paine opened his eyes to darkness and the conviction that he had died.

Then the hull groaned. Someone snored. A lantern rocked at the end of the passage. Death, he thought, was unlikely to smell so strongly of onions.

He tried to laugh, but produced only a dry click. A sailor heard it and came near.

“Still with us, Mr. Paine?”

“Against advice.”

“Save your wit. You’ve little enough strength.”

The sailor lifted his head and gave him a few drops of water. Paine’s beard was stiff with sweat. His shirt clung to him. He could not raise his own hand. The knowledge frightened him more than the fever dreams. He had always trusted motion: walking, working, talking, beginning again. Now his body lay beyond command, a failed instrument.

Days later—the count had become uncertain—a cry traveled down from the deck.

Land.

Those who could move climbed up. Those who could not waited for reports. Paine heard the words capes, river, pilot. The sea’s violence eased, replaced by the shorter pulse of the Delaware. Cold air entered through an opened hatch carrying smells he could not separate: mud, pine smoke, marsh, perhaps only imagination.

America announced itself to him first as stillness.

On November 30, 1774, the *London Packet* reached Philadelphia. The other passengers disembarked among shouted names, thumping baggage, and reunions on the wharf. Paine remained in his berth. He heard feet depart until the ship seemed unnaturally hollow.

At last two men appeared with a sling and blanket. A physician had been summoned by people to whom Franklin’s letter had been shown. Paine protested when they lifted him, mostly from shame.

“You may walk if you prefer,” one of them said.

Paine looked down at his legs, which seemed to belong to a much older man. “On reflection, I have become an admirer of transport.”

They carried him into daylight.

Philadelphia swam above him: brick warehouses, steep roofs, masts, faces, a pale sky crossed by gulls. Wheels rattled over stones. Bells sounded somewhere beyond the riverfront. People spoke with accents English enough to understand and altered enough to hear. He caught only fragments before weakness closed his eyes.

The new world received him flat on his back.

For weeks his America measured no larger than a rented room. There was a bed, a chair, a washstand, and a window through which winter light advanced and withdrew. The physician came. Broth appeared. Sheets were changed by a woman who refused conversation when Paine was weak and refused silence when he was strong enough to complain. His money dwindled while his body repaired itself by humiliating increments.

He read Franklin’s letter again when his hands stopped trembling. Richard Bache came to see him, first out of obligation and then, Paine thought, out of interest. Bache was a merchant and Franklin’s son-in-law, younger than the famous man’s reputation made him seem. He examined the gaunt traveler who had arrived under such peculiar recommendation.

“Dr. Franklin describes you as ingenious,” Bache said.

“He was writing from England. Our standards have declined.”

Bache smiled. “Can you keep accounts?”

“Badly enough to understand their tyranny.”

“Can you write?”

“Better than I keep accounts.”

“That may be useful here.”

Through Bache, newspapers began to reach the room. Paine read them slowly, tiring after a column, returning after sleep. The pages gave him a country before he could walk through the city: resolutions of the recent Continental Congress; agreements not to import British goods; speeches from Massachusetts; replies from governors; advertisements for runaway servants and enslaved people; notices of ships, auctions, remedies, books, land, and lost horses. High principle and daily commerce crowded together without embarrassment.

The Congress had adjourned only weeks before Paine arrived, but its presence lingered. Delegates from twelve colonies had sat in Philadelphia and discovered that grievances separated by hundreds of miles might be spoken in one room. They had professed loyalty to the king while organizing resistance to Parliament. They had petitioned, resolved, and agreed to stop trade. Every sentence appeared to advance with one foot and retreat with the other.

Paine recognized the posture. It was the stance of men approaching a door they feared to open.

By January he could walk to the end of the street. The city shocked him with its order. Philadelphia did not sprawl like London beneath layers of soot and memory. Its streets crossed at right angles. Brick houses stood with scrubbed steps and shutters. Signs swung over shops: booksellers, saddlers, cabinetmakers, apothecaries, printers. Sleigh bells sounded after snow. Market women stamped their feet against the cold beside baskets of roots and apples.

Yet beneath the plain facades ran restless argument. At the London Coffee House, merchants bent over shipping lists and cursed the nonimportation agreement they publicly supported. In taverns, artisans debated whether Massachusetts had gone too far or Britain not far enough. Quaker elders urged peace. Young men drilled with a new seriousness. Broadsides appeared on walls and vanished beneath replies.

Paine moved through it hungry for everything. He asked questions with the heedlessness of an outsider and listened with the concentration of a man whose life depended on understanding. In England, class announced the limits of a conversation before it began. Here those limits had not disappeared, but they seemed less settled. A prosperous mechanic might dispute with a merchant; a farmer might print an argument addressed to a governor. Titles carried weight, though not always enough to end a sentence.

One afternoon Bache took him to meet Robert Aitken.

Aitken’s bookshop smelled of paper, leather, ink, and coal smoke. Volumes stood behind glass while pamphlets lay in practical stacks. In the printing room beyond, the press came down with a wooden groan, lifted, and came down again. A young pressman worked the bar; another handled damp sheets with blackened fingers. The noise was regular as a great mechanical heart.

Aitken himself was a compact Scotsman with wary eyes and the compressed energy of a man conducting three businesses with money sufficient for two. He had come from Edinburgh years earlier, established himself as bookseller and printer, and now intended to launch an American magazine. Others had attempted such ventures. Most had expired after a few issues, killed by distance, expense, tardy subscribers, and the public’s remarkable ability to desire literature without paying for it.

Aitken examined Paine over the rim of his spectacles.

“Mr. Bache says you have written for print.”

Paine gave him a copy of *The Case of the Officers of Excise*, worn from travel. Aitken turned the pages, pausing at the passages Paine had labored hardest to make plain.

“You understand figures,” Aitken said.

“When they represent injustice.”

“I need an editor, not an agitator.”

“The distinction depends upon what is submitted.”

Aitken looked up sharply. Then, despite himself, he laughed.

He described the proposed *Pennsylvania Magazine*. It would contain useful knowledge and agreeable entertainment: natural philosophy, history, mechanics, poetry, descriptions of places, accounts of inventions. It must appeal to learned readers without excluding ordinary ones. It must be original enough to possess character and varied enough to justify a subscription.

“Can you select material?” Aitken asked.

“Yes.”

“Correct it?”

“Yes.”

“Supply what is missing?”

“Almost certainly.”

“Meet a date?”

Paine hesitated a fraction too long.

“Dates,” Aitken said, “are the bones of a periodical.”

“Then I shall endeavor not to cripple it.”

They agreed on fifty pounds a year, not wealth but rescue. Paine left with an occupation, a stack of submissions, and ink on his thumb. In the street he stopped to look back through the shop window. The press descended. A fresh sheet emerged. Ideas, which entered the room invisible, left in hundreds.

He had found his machine.

The first months demanded every faculty he possessed. Copy arrived in cramped hands, on scraps, in packets, and sometimes not at all. Paine cut pompous essays to half their length, corrected claims he suspected were false, and hunted through borrowed books to fill blank pages. He translated science into language a cabinetmaker might read after supper. He solicited verse, rejected worse verse, composed notices, and argued over engravings Aitken said cost too much.

At the press, language became physical. Each letter had to be chosen from a case and set backward in a composing stick. A page existed first as rows of raised metal, tightened into a form. Ink was beaten over type; paper was laid; the bar was pulled. One misplaced letter might reproduce its error a thousand times. Paine liked the discipline. A writer could indulge vagueness in his head, but type demanded a choice.

He learned Aitken’s calculations too: paper by the ream, labor by the day, postage by distance, subscribers by the probability of payment. The editor’s imagination and the printer’s solvency wrestled over every issue.

“Two more pages,” Paine urged once, defending an account of a new mechanical device.

“Two pages do not descend from heaven,” Aitken said. “They are made of paper.”

“Then consider it an investment in human improvement.”

“Human improvement owes me twelve pounds already.”

The February issue went out beneath the full title *The Pennsylvania Magazine: or, American Monthly Museum*. Paine held an uncut copy while the ink was still rich and dark. The word *American* struck him. The magazine did not call itself Philadelphian, Pennsylvanian, or British American. It claimed a whole continent for its room.

In his opening address he considered what a magazine might be in a country still forming its habits of thought. It could gather knowledge scattered across distances, encourage invention, refine taste, and give useful ideas a common home. Beneath those respectable purposes lay another that he did not state. A magazine could teach separate provinces to imagine themselves as one public.

Readers responded. Subscriptions rose. Letters came from beyond the city. Some praised. Some corrected. Some enclosed manuscripts whose authors plainly believed that paper had become precious chiefly so their opinions might occupy it. Paine worked late in a small room, a candle guttering beside his elbow, and felt for the first time not that he was pleading at the edge of public life but that he had entered its current.

He wrote under names other than his own. An editor who filled gaps too visibly risked making the magazine a monologue, so Paine became a small crowd. One voice offered reflections; another told an anecdote; another examined a social cruelty with feigned surprise. The masks freed him. Thomas Paine had failed in business, marriage, teaching, corset-making, and excise service. *Atlanticus*, when he chose that signature, had failed at nothing. Atlanticus belonged to the ocean between worlds and could speak from neither shore.

His subjects wandered because his curiosity did. He wrote of science, of useful inventions, of courage, of conduct, of the folly of dueling. He considered the misery created when human beings treated custom as moral proof. He returned repeatedly to those whom respectable writing kept at its margins: the poor, women trapped by unequal law, animals tormented for amusement, Africans held in bondage in a land proclaiming liberty.

Slavery confronted him everywhere once he learned to see it. Notices in the newspapers described men and women as property and detailed their scars so that they might be captured. Ships unloaded goods produced by coerced labor. Men denounced Parliament for attempting to enslave America while holding other human beings in literal chains.

At a tavern, Paine heard a merchant use that comparison and interrupted.

“You say Britain would make slaves of you.”

“That is precisely their design.”

“Then you know slavery to be an abomination.”

“Of course.”

“In all climates?”

The merchant’s expression hardened. “The situations are not comparable.”

“They are not. One is a figure of speech.”

The table went still. Bache, seated nearby, watched him with a mixture of warning and approval.

Outside afterward, Bache said, “You have a gift for ending agreeable evenings.”

“Agreement is not always a sign that an evening has been useful.”

“In Philadelphia, you will find that moral clarity often sends an invoice.”

Paine knew it. Printers depended on subscribers; merchants purchased advertisements; patrons expected discretion. Even abolitionists disagreed over how directly to indict men whose support they needed in other causes. But the contradiction lodged under his skin. The same natural right could not be sacred facing east and invisible facing south.

In March, an antislavery essay signed “Justice and Humanity” appeared in the *Pennsylvania Journal*, condemning the trade and exposing the hypocrisy of colonial complaints. People discussed its authorship. Some laid it at Paine’s door; others named different hands. He did not satisfy their curiosity. What mattered was not the name beneath an argument but whether the reader could answer it.

That spring, politics overtook every other subject.

On an April morning riders brought news from Massachusetts: British regulars had marched to seize colonial military stores at Concord. Men had faced them at Lexington. Shots had been fired. Americans were dead.

The story arrived in fragments, enlarged and corrected as each express reached the city. Crowds gathered at the State House and outside newspaper offices. Aitken’s shop filled until customers blocked the pressroom door. Someone read an account aloud. At the sentence describing bodies on Lexington green, a woman near the counter covered her mouth.

“Who fired first?” a man demanded.

No one could say.

“It matters,” another insisted.

Paine stood beside a rack of drying sheets and thought that it mattered less with every mile the news traveled. Whatever finger had tightened first, government troops and colonial farmers had exchanged fire. Blood had supplied its own argument.

Philadelphia changed within days. Associators drilled in yards and open spaces. Gunsmiths worked longer hours. Powder became a subject of general expertise among men who had never considered its manufacture. Pacifists warned against the fever of retaliation. Loyalists lowered their voices. The colonies prepared for war while declaring that reconciliation remained their object.

Paine attended meetings and listened. The words *rights*, *liberty*, and *constitution* appeared in every speech, yet speakers used them as if their meanings were universally settled. He began asking the impolite question beneath the debate.

Reconciliation with what?

With a Parliament that claimed unlimited authority? With ministers who sent armies to enforce it? With a king whose virtue patriots preserved by assigning every royal act they disliked to wicked advisers? The colonists cursed branches while kneeling to the root.

“The king may yet intervene,” one acquaintance told him.

“He has intervened,” Paine said. “Those are his troops.”

“His ministers mislead him.”

“Then he is too weak to govern.”

“You mistake the constitution.”

“No. I am attempting to find it.”

He searched histories of England and found not a balanced temple of king, lords, and commons but a long compromise among conquests, privileges, accidents, and force. Americans revered the British constitution partly because they viewed it from a distance. The machine’s noise softened across the ocean. Paine had stood closer. He had watched patronage reward obedience and hereditary power shelter incompetence.

Still, he did not yet publish the conclusion forming in him. The word *independence* carried the smell of treason. Even radicals denied desiring it. Congress, assembled again in Philadelphia, raised an army and appointed George Washington to command it, then sent the Olive Branch Petition to the king. Men made war with one hand and extended peace with the other.

Through the summer the city saw delegates in the streets, officers riding north, wagons loaded with supplies. News came of Bunker Hill: terrible British losses, American retreat, courage on both sides, no path backward visible. Paine imagined the slope above Boston, smoke hiding men who only months before had called one another countrymen.

The magazine continued. It had to. Readers still wanted discoveries, histories, and poems even as armies gathered. Aitken insisted that politics not consume every page.

“A magazine must survive the crisis,” he said.

“The crisis may not permit anything to stand outside it,” Paine answered.

Their arguments sharpened. Aitken wanted regular copy and commercial prudence. Paine wanted room to follow the moral pressure of events. He could be erratic with hours, especially when conversation at a tavern carried him past midnight, and Aitken could reduce every inspiration to its cost in paper. Each thought the other insufficiently grateful.

Yet in the friction Paine learned the craft he would need. He learned how long an argument could be before a reader set it down. He learned that plain words traveled farther than elegant ones. He learned to place an image where logic might otherwise pass unheard. Most important, he learned that common readers did not require ideas to be made small. They required them to be made clear.

One evening he drafted a reflection on monarchy, then stopped with the pen above the page. Rain tapped the window. From the printing room came the faint metallic sorting of type.

He had written *the absurdity of hereditary wisdom*.

The phrase pleased him, but pleasure was a warning. It sounded composed. A dockworker would not repeat it.

He crossed out *the absurdity of hereditary wisdom* and wrote: *One honest man is of more worth than all the crowned ruffians that ever lived.*

He stared at the sentence. It was too fierce for the piece before him. It accused not a minister but the institution itself. He folded the sheet and put it in his pocket.

By autumn, the king had rejected the colonies’ petition and proclaimed them in rebellion. News of the proclamation reached Philadelphia with reports of more ships, more troops, and the burning of Falmouth in Massachusetts. A town could be destroyed by the government to which its inhabitants still professed loyalty. The fiction of a family quarrel grew harder to maintain.

Paine walked to the Delaware on cold evenings. Ships swung at anchor, their rigging black against the dusk. Less than a year earlier, one had delivered him half-dead to this shore. He thought of England now without the ache that had accompanied departure. Distance had clarified rather than erased it. The country of his birth was not the same thing as its government. To oppose a king was not to hate a people.

Behind him, Philadelphia’s lamps appeared one by one. Thirteen colonies stretched beyond the city, diverse, suspicious, accustomed to looking toward London more often than toward one another. They had no single government except a Congress that insisted it was temporary, no monarch, no settled constitution, no shared name that all used with confidence.

They had printers.

They had roads, post riders, taverns, committees, congregations, marketplaces, and men who read aloud to men who could not read. An argument placed in Philadelphia might be heard in a Connecticut farmhouse, a Carolina ordinary, a camp outside Boston. The mechanism was already built. It waited for words equal to the distance.

Paine took Franklin’s old letter from his coat. The folds had softened; the edges were worn. He remembered the sickroom, the narrow window, Bache asking whether he could write. He had crossed the Atlantic believing America might offer him employment. He had not understood that it might offer him a subject.

Footsteps approached over the wharf. Dr. Benjamin Rush drew alongside him, his face sharp in the cold. The physician had become a valued acquaintance, one of those men who combined public seriousness with private urgency. Rush had read Paine’s work and heard enough of his conversation to recognize where it tended.

“You have been thinking,” Rush said.

“I have acquired the habit.”

“About the king.”

Paine looked out at the dark river. “About the inconvenience of kings.”

Rush lowered his voice though no one stood near. “That is a dangerous inconvenience to discuss.”

“Danger is not an answer.”

“No. But it determines how an answer must be given.”

They walked. Rush spoke of the public mood, the attachment many still felt toward Britain, the dread of disorder, the certainty that any open call for separation would be denounced. Paine listened. Every objection was real. None altered the structure of the problem.

“People do not yet want independence,” Rush said.

“They want what independence would give them.”

“That is not the same.”

“Only because no one has made it the same.”

Rush stopped. Across the river, darkness covered the Jersey shore.

“Then perhaps someone must.”

The sentence passed quietly between them. No crowd heard it. No press marked the moment. The harbor continued its creaking conversation with tide and wind.

Paine felt the old instinct to petition rise and fall away. He would not beg the king to become wise. He would not decorate submission with the language of rights. The case must begin farther back, before Parliament, before charters, before the Norman conquest and all the antique debris men called constitution. It must ask what government was for, where authority came from, and why an island claimed dominion over a continent.

It must not sound like a scholar addressing scholars. It must enter a workshop without bowing and sit beside the fire in a farmhouse. It must give ordinary people words for the conclusion already advancing through their lives.

The wind off the Delaware cut through his coat. Paine put a hand over Franklin’s letter, then over the folded sentence he had saved.

England had taught him how power defended itself. The ocean had stripped him to breath and bone. Philadelphia had given him a press, a public, and a country on the edge of naming itself.

He turned from the river toward the city.

Winter was coming, and Thomas Paine had found his voice.
