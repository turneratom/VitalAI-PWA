# Chapter 10: What Remains

By the spring of 1809, Thomas Paine’s world had narrowed to a room.

Outside, New York shook itself awake each morning with wheels, bells, boots, hawkers, river traffic, and the hard music of commerce. The city was climbing northward, consuming gardens and lanes, turning fields into prospects. Nations did the same thing at greater scale and called it progress. Paine could hear the future being built beyond his window and could no longer walk out to inspect it.

Inside, time moved by smaller instruments: a spoon against a cup, medicine cooling on a table, footsteps on stairs, the interval between pain and sleep.

He lay in the care of Marguerite de Bonneville near Greenwich Village, his body swollen by illness and years. Gout had besieged his joints. Weakness made every shift in bed a decision. The face that caricaturists had enlarged into moral warning was now simply an old man’s face: gray, sore, alert in flashes, defenseless in sleep.

He was seventy-two. The number startled visitors who remembered a voice without age.

The room held papers because every room Paine inhabited eventually did. Letters, drafts, printed attacks, notes on theology, scraps of invention, accounts no one had reconciled. A life devoted to reducing vast ideas into plain sentences had produced an untidy republic of paper. Some sheets mattered. Some were repetitions, grievances, corrections to corrections. All bore the pressure of a hand that had believed writing was action.

Marguerite tried to impose order upon the living before sorting the words. She opened the window when the air turned stale. She shooed away the merely curious. She brought broth, argued over blankets, and endured the old man’s suspicion that care was a conspiracy against independence.

“I can hold it,” he said when she lifted a cup.

His hand proved otherwise.

She did not say so. She let his fingers close around it while hers quietly carried the weight.

That compromise—his gesture, her strength—was the last form dignity often took.

The city knew he was dying. News traveled through political clubs, churches, newspaper offices, and taverns with differing amounts of pity. To freethinkers he was a patriarch under siege. To old republicans he was an uncomfortable debt. To clergymen he was a final opportunity. Deathbeds were public stages in an age that treated last words as verdicts, and Thomas Paine’s soul had become contested property before it had left him.

Religious visitors came.

Some came from concern. Others came for conquest. If the author of *The Age of Reason* could be made to recant, every page he had written might be declared defeated by one frightened sentence. Believers who would never have permitted him to speak from their pulpits now wanted exclusive rights to his whisper.

Two ministers arrived together on a wet afternoon. Their coats darkened the hall. Marguerite blocked the door to his room.

“He is sleeping.”

“Madam, the matter cannot wait.”

“Then your God has chosen poor timing.”

One minister colored. The other lowered his voice, as if gentleness might pass where authority could not. “We wish only to offer consolation.”

From the bed came Paine’s rough call. “Consolation rarely requires two witnesses.”

They entered.

The ministers stood near the foot of the bed, careful not to sit among the papers. Paine watched them with eyes made bright by fever. They asked whether he wished to make peace with Jesus Christ. They urged him not to face eternity carrying the burden of unbelief.

Paine breathed slowly before answering.

“I have no quarrel with your Jesus,” he said. “It is his proprietors who trouble me.”

The younger man opened a Bible. “Sir, at this hour—”

“At this hour I remain the man I was at noon.”

They prayed despite his refusal. The words filled the room, polished and impersonal. Paine turned his face toward the wall. Marguerite watched the ministers watching him, each alert for surrender.

None came.

When they left, they carried ambiguity into the street. Ambiguity would be improved in retelling. One witness would later remember a groan as repentance, another a refusal as proof of damnation. Stories about Paine’s final moments multiplied because each faction required a death compatible with its argument.

Paine had spent his life resisting kings, conventions, churches, and parties that claimed the right to speak for other people. He was not likely to surrender that right at the boundary of consciousness.

Still, defiance did not make dying easy.

At night, pain stripped philosophy to nerve. He called out. He asked for water, then pushed it away. He drifted through rooms that no longer existed. Marguerite heard him speak names from France and America, fragments from an assembly, instructions to a printer. Once he demanded that someone shut the prison door because the mark was on the wrong side. Once he argued with Washington in a voice barely louder than cloth.

Then lucidity returned and humiliation with it.

“How long?” he asked.

Marguerite knew what he meant. “No one knows.”

“Everyone knows. They are being polite with arithmetic.”

She adjusted the pillow. “Then disappoint them.”

His mouth found the remnant of a smile.

He had always disappointed predictions. A stay-maker’s son was not supposed to advise continents. An excise officer dismissed from government service was not supposed to name a nation’s desire. A pamphleteer was not supposed to survive two revolutions, much less three governments’ suspicion. A prisoner marked for execution was not supposed to wake after the Terror and demand his seat back.

But mortality was not a ministry that could be petitioned.

Friends came in small number. Some sat briefly, embarrassed by the body’s betrayal. They spoke too loudly, as if age had made him deaf rather than ill. They repeated news from Washington, Europe, the docks. Bonaparte was remaking the map. Britain still fought. America quarreled over trade, expansion, and honor. The republic continued its unfinished argument.

Paine listened and sometimes flared awake.

“And slavery?” he asked one visitor.

The man hesitated. “Still with us.”

“Then do not say the Revolution is finished.”

The effort exhausted him. He closed his eyes, but one finger continued to tap the blanket as though keeping place in debate.

Others stayed away. They had reasons: distance, illness, business, theological offense, old quarrels, concern for reputation. Reasons form an orderly procession behind every abandoned person. Paine, who had written for millions, could count his intimate attendants without reaching the second hand.

He knew.

Neglect did not surprise him anymore, but it still injured. Age does not cure the need to be remembered by those one remembers.

On better mornings he asked for documents from New Rochelle. Property had to be settled. Marguerite and her children had to be provided for. The farm—gift of New York, refuge, burden, symbol—must pass according to his wishes. He dictated, corrected, changed his mind, grew angry when fatigue blurred a phrase.

“Enough,” Marguerite would say.

“Enough is a word executors use.”

“Enough is a word used by the woman holding the ink.”

He glared. She capped the bottle. An hour later they resumed.

His will carried affection in the language available to a guarded man: land, goods, money, instructions. He had never built a conventional family that could gather naturally around his bed. The Bonneville household, with all its tensions and disputed interpretations, received the practical shape of his loyalty.

The great advocate of universal rights confronted the most private transfer of all: what one human being leaves to particular others.

He owned little compared with the reach of his fame. The farm had value, but his life had not been arranged for accumulation. *Common Sense* had sold in numbers astonishing for the colonies, and Paine had surrendered or refused much of its profit for the cause. He had been rewarded by legislatures, employed, celebrated, and still repeatedly approached poverty. Money passed through him toward print, travel, models, campaigns, friends, and bad arrangements.

Had he kept the ordinary author’s share from the revolution, he might have died surrounded by furniture and cousins.

He had preferred independence to royalties.

There was no grandeur in the room. Candle wax leaned over its holder. A basin waited. Linen dried near the hearth. The chamber pot did not recognize genius. Mortality is a radical democrat.

From the bed, Paine could sometimes see a piece of sky. Clouds crossed it without historical consciousness. He watched them when speech became expensive. Perhaps he remembered the Atlantic, though no one could know. The first crossing in 1774, sick almost to death before America had even seen him. The return in 1802, older, notorious, carrying Jefferson’s invitation. Water had separated each of his lives: England, America, France, America again.

Now the far shore offered no visible coast.

On June 7 the room grew warmer. His breathing changed. Those attending him recognized the alteration before admitting it. Messages were sent. The city continued.

Paine moved between wakefulness and a place language could not follow. Marguerite sat nearby. A physician came and could offer observation disguised as treatment. The remedies of the day had accompanied him as far as their small science allowed.

Someone asked again whether he believed Jesus Christ to be the Son of God.

The question entered a room already crowded with death.

Paine’s answer has been carried through history in competing versions. In one, he firmly refuses the belief. In another, he says he has no wish to believe on the subject. Devout storytellers later invented terror, cries for mercy, a recantation suppressed by infidels. Admirers polished his composure until he seemed to die like an argument rather than a man.

The honest image is harder and more human: a suffering old radical, pressed for ideological tribute, retaining enough of himself not to counterfeit conviction.

Near morning on June 8, 1809, the breath stopped returning.

No thunder marked it. No press halted. No government chamber rose.

Thomas Paine died in New York while the republic went about its business.

For a moment the room possessed the peculiar stillness that follows a death expected for days and believed only when it arrives. The watchers waited for another breath. The body, so argumentative in life, made no appeal.

Marguerite closed his eyes.

On the table lay pages no one had yet decided were history.

News moved outward. Notices appeared. Some were respectful, some barbed, some unable to mention *Common Sense* without balancing it against *The Age of Reason*, as though death required an account book with patriotism on one side and unbelief on the other. The nation had no agreed language for mourning a man it had turned into two incompatible men.

The funeral exposed that division with brutal economy.

Paine had expressed a desire to be buried in a Quaker burial ground. His father’s faith had stayed somewhere in him—not its institutional gatekeeping, but its plainness, its silence, its refusal of ornamental hierarchy. He wanted earth without theater.

The Quakers refused.

He was not a member in acceptable standing. His religious writings and manner of belief placed him beyond their boundary. The son of a Quaker stay-maker, whose political philosophy carried traces of the Inner Light translated into universal rights, could not enter the ground.

Institutions often become most certain at graves.

With the desired burial denied, the body was taken north to New Rochelle. The journey retraced the narrowing geography of his final years. Roads jarred the coffin. Fields opened. The farm waited, green with early summer, indifferent to doctrinal verdict.

Only a handful followed.

Accounts differ over the exact number and identities, but the smallness is beyond dispute. Marguerite was there. Her sons were there. A few others—neighbors, friends, people whose loyalty outweighed the weather of reputation—stood near the grave. No president attended. No delegation came from Congress. No state whose independence he had served supplied official honor.

Perhaps six mourners. Perhaps slightly more. Not enough to carry the fame of the dead without feeling its disproportion.

They buried him at the edge of his farm beneath a walnut tree, close to the road. Soil struck the coffin with the oldest sound in human ritual. There was no monument worthy of the sentences that had helped make a nation, only earth, sky, leaves, and the uncomfortable living.

One can imagine the road continuing past while they stood there. A wagon. A rider. Someone with no idea whom the ground had taken.

That was the first burial of Thomas Paine.

The republic did not end. Elections came. Parties shifted names. Children learned a cleaner Revolution populated by powdered founders who agreed more often than they had. Paine appeared when useful: author of *Common Sense*, voice of the crisis, champion of independence. Then the textbook door closed before France, poverty, deism, social welfare, and rejection entered.

Memory made him presentable by making him partial.

At New Rochelle, the grave endured ordinary assaults. Weather softened the mound. Visitors chipped at relics. The farm changed hands. A stone marked the spot, but no national shrine grew around it. Paine’s body lay in land given to him for helping create the country that had nearly forgotten where it put him.

Then William Cobbett came.

Cobbett was an English journalist, pamphleteer, farmer, political combatant, and manufacturer of prose sharp enough to shave with. In earlier years he had attacked Paine viciously. Writing under the name Peter Porcupine in America, he had helped create the contempt surrounding the radical’s return. Cobbett had called him degraded, dangerous, contemptible—contributing his own ink to the atmosphere in which old friends stepped away.

Time and English politics rearranged Cobbett’s judgment. He came to admire the man he had abused, especially Paine’s courage against corruption and aristocratic power. The conversion possessed the intensity of guilt. Praise was not enough. Cobbett wanted restitution visible enough to rebuke a nation.

In 1819, ten years after Paine’s death, he went to New Rochelle with a plan as audacious as it was macabre: remove Paine’s remains, carry them to England, and raise a grand monument by public subscription.

The dead man who had crossed the Atlantic twice would cross once more.

Cobbett chose the night. Whether secrecy arose from legal uncertainty, local resistance, or theatrical instinct, darkness suited the act. Men opened the grave. Spades entered the soil where the few mourners had stood. The coffin, decayed by a decade underground, yielded what remained.

Bone came into lantern light.

The image is almost too exact a symbol: Thomas Paine, who had argued that no generation owned the next, exhumed by a later polemicist determined to revise the verdict of the previous one.

Cobbett gathered the bones. Reports mention the skull and larger pieces, the remnants placed into a box or trunk for transport. Earth was closed over emptiness. New Rochelle woke with a grave but no occupant.

When challenged, Cobbett did not deny the act. He proclaimed it. England, he said, should honor the great Englishman America had neglected. He would build a monument. He would turn shame into public gratitude.

The plan failed.

Subscriptions did not rise as he imagined. Politics intervened. Enthusiasm thinned. A country does not repair neglect merely because one energetic man carries a box of bones across the ocean. Paine’s remains stayed with Cobbett, moving through the clutter of a restless life.

Cobbett died in 1835.

Afterward, certainty dissolved.

The bones were reportedly dispersed, lost, sold, passed among people who did not preserve a reliable chain. Claims appeared. A skull here, a hand there, fragments attached to stories and institutions. None produced a complete, authenticated body. The physical remains of one of the clearest political writers in English became an unsolved sentence.

There is a grim comedy Paine might have appreciated. Churches that accused him of materialism guarded empty certainties; admirers searching for relics imitated the pieties he distrusted. The man who insisted reason mattered more than sacred objects became an object no one could locate.

His New Rochelle monument marks a burial from which the buried was removed.

His bones, like his citizenship, were denied a settled country.

Yet to ask what remains and answer “nothing” is to misunderstand both bodies and books.

Paine’s physical disappearance sharpened the other evidence. *Common Sense* remained. *The American Crisis* remained. *Rights of Man*, *The Age of Reason*, *Agrarian Justice*, letters, arguments, bridge designs, insults, errors, prophecies—these survived not as relics but as active materials. They entered new readers and caused trouble.

His legacy refused the order of a monument because it operated more like fire.

A monument tells people where to stand. Fire moves.

Paine’s sentences crossed borders he could not. Radicals in Britain read him while demanding parliamentary reform. Working people found in his prose an argument addressed to their intelligence rather than delivered over their heads. Abolitionists recognized the universal language that the American republic had failed to honor. Freethinkers inherited his attack on priestly authority. Advocates of public support could look back to his proposals for old-age pensions, grants to young adults, and aid funded by taxing inherited wealth.

He had written before modern ideological labels settled into their camps. Later generations therefore tried to enlist him selectively. Nationalists claimed the patriot. Internationalists claimed the citizen of the world. Liberals claimed natural rights. Democrats claimed popular sovereignty. Secularists claimed religious criticism. Social reformers claimed economic justice. Each could quote him. None could comfortably own the whole.

That was fitting.

Paine had never been good property.

His central faith was not in a party or even a nation. It was in the capacity of ordinary people to judge claims made against them. He wrote plainly not because his ideas were simple, but because obscurity was often authority’s bodyguard. Monarchy survived through costume, inherited habit, and language designed to make theft sound sacred. Priestcraft survived through exclusive access to mystery. Social cruelty survived by naming itself order.

Paine stripped the names away.

“A long habit of not thinking a thing wrong,” he had written, “gives it a superficial appearance of being right.” The sentence outlived every politician it accused. It remains dangerous because habit remains politically useful.

His limitations remained too. He could be vain, rash, unjust in anger, careless with friendship, overconfident in reason’s immediate victory. He underestimated the human appetite for belonging, ritual, and inherited identity. He sometimes wrote as though exposing contradiction would cause power to release its grip from embarrassment. Power is rarely embarrassed for long.

He was not a secular saint.

He would have mistrusted the category.

His greatness lay closer to use than purity. At three decisive thresholds, he said what respectable men considered premature. America should not petition for better treatment but become independent. Rights belonged to people, not ranks or nations. Poverty amid civilization was a political creation requiring public remedy. Religion must answer to individual reason. Revolution must not reproduce the tyranny it overthrew.

These positions cost him because he did not hold them sequentially for applause. He held them together.

America honored many founders with capitals, currencies, estates preserved behind ropes, and myths buffed smooth by repetition. Paine received a roadside grave emptied by an English antagonist. The imbalance says less about the scale of his contribution than about the kind of contribution nations find easy to celebrate.

Founders who become institutions can be domesticated. Paine remained a question asked of institutions.

Who gave you the right?

Whom does your order serve?

What suffering have you taught people to call natural?

Would your principle still apply to your enemy, your servant, your slave, the unbeliever at your door?

Those questions do not sit quietly beneath marble.

They walk.

Years after the exhumation, people still came to the New Rochelle site. They stood by the monument and looked at the ground. Some came as pilgrims, some as historians, some because an odd story about missing bones had caught their interest. The absence below became part of the memorial.

But the more faithful pilgrimage happened elsewhere and without announcement.

It happened whenever a reader opened Paine for the first time.

Imagine such a reader—not any single historical person, but the recurring citizen for whom the work was written. A printer’s apprentice in a back room. A factory worker after a fourteen-hour day. A woman excluded from the vote but not from judgment. An enslaved man hearing the language of natural equality and measuring the nation against it. An immigrant learning the claims of a republic. A student told the founders were statues and discovering that one of them wrote like a door being kicked open.

The setting changes. The gesture does not.

A hand finds the cover.

The pages give a little at the spine. Perhaps the book is old and foxed, perhaps newly printed, perhaps glowing on a screen Paine could not have imagined. The title appears: *Common Sense*.

No trumpet sounds. There is no guide standing by to separate the acceptable Paine from the dangerous one. The reader enters alone.

“Society in every state is a blessing,” the voice begins, “but government, even in its best state, is but a necessary evil.”

The syntax is eighteenth-century, but the address is immediate. The writer does not bow to the reader or bully him with pedigree. He assumes she can think. He places kings beneath examination and inherited power beneath contempt. He takes what custom made immense and turns it in the light until its joints show.

The reader looks up.

This is the instant that monuments cannot contain.

In 1776, Paine understood that a political transformation had to occur in language before it could occur in law. People could not choose independence while imagining monarchy as nature. The pamphlet’s achievement was not to invent colonial grievance; grievance was everywhere. It was to alter the scale of the possible. Separation ceased to sound mad. The island ruling the continent began to sound mad.

Every new reading repeats that reversal in miniature. A structure accepted as permanent becomes made. What is made can be unmade. What is inherited can be judged. What is judged can be refused.

The person who wrote those sentences has no known grave containing his body.

He does not need one for this meeting.

The reader turns a page. Outside, some modern republic goes about its business, congratulating itself, excluding someone, refining old privilege into new procedure. Officials invoke necessity. Preachers invoke certainty. Wealth invokes merit. Habit puts on a judge’s robe.

Then Paine’s voice enters—not flawless, not gentle, not safely dead.

It asks whether the thing called inevitable is merely old.

It asks whether reverence has been earned.

It asks what right one person possesses to rule another without consent.

The questions carry the heat of the pressroom. Two and a half centuries collapse into the slight resistance of paper beneath a thumb.

On the last morning of his life, Paine’s hand could not reliably lift a cup. After his death, men refused him a Quaker grave. A decade later, Cobbett lifted his bones from American earth and carried them into disappearance. Institutions failed him in sequence: party, church, nation, monument, archive.

The words escaped each failure.

Somewhere, now, a new reader bends closer.

The room is quiet enough to hear a page turn.

The grave in New Rochelle is empty. The box in England is lost. The Atlantic keeps no record of what crossed it. But the sentence on the page has arrived intact, black and urgent, waiting for no anniversary.

The reader touches the margin.

The ink looks wet.
