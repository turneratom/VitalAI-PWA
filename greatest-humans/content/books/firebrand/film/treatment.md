# FIREBRAND

## Feature Film Treatment

**Based on the book by Brad Turner**

**Genre:** Historical drama  
**Running time:** Approximately 120 minutes

### Logline

An obscure English craftsman arrives in America with nothing but a letter of introduction and a gift for plain, incendiary prose, then risks friendship, freedom, and his place in history as his words ignite the American and French Revolutions—and as the republic he helped create turns against him for refusing to stop thinking freely.

### Tone and visual language

*Firebrand* is intimate history on an epic canvas: urgent, tactile, unsentimental, and darkly funny. Thomas Paine is neither marble founder nor secular saint. He is brilliant, difficult, generous, vain, physically untidy, morally exacting, and unable to leave power unchallenged even when silence would save him.

The recurring palette is **ink, snow, candle, Atlantic gray, and French stone**. Ink begins as labor—type stained into fingertips—then becomes bloodless gunpowder. Snow defines the American crisis, bleaching the landscape while soldiers leave red footprints. Candlelight makes rooms feel conspiratorial and faces unstable. The Atlantic is gray both times Paine crosses it, a border between selves. French stone evolves from enlightened architecture to prison wall. Warmth is rare and therefore meaningful: firelight around ordinary readers, Franklin’s smile, a child beside Paine’s bridge model.

The camera stays close to work: hands setting type, stitching shoes, loading muskets, folding pamphlets, voting in assemblies, chalking prison doors, digging a grave. Great events arrive through physical choices. Paine’s language is heard sparingly in voice-over; more often, his printed words migrate into the mouths of readers.

## Act I — The spark (0–35 minutes)

In 1809, an elderly Paine lies dying in a cramped New York room while ministers press him to recant. He turns toward the wall. On the table, a fly crosses a copy of *Common Sense*. The scrape of its legs becomes the scrape of a stay-maker’s tool.

Thetford, 1740s. Young Thomas works beneath his Quaker father Joseph, absorbing both the dignity of labor and the humiliation of class. A public hanging, a recruiter’s drum, and a customer’s contempt teach him that authority survives by costume. Adult Paine stumbles through failed trades, a dead marriage, and government work as an excise officer. He petitions Parliament for fair pay, is dismissed, and reaches London nearly ruined.

In a bookshop he challenges Benjamin Franklin during a discussion about the colonies. Franklin recognizes an undisciplined mind of unusual force and gives him a letter to America. Paine crosses the Atlantic in 1774, nearly dying of fever, and arrives in Philadelphia carried off the ship.

Print culture revives him. Working with editor Robert Aitken, he writes against slavery and watches colonial resistance harden after Lexington and Concord. At a tavern, patriots demand reconciliation with Britain. Paine asks why a continent should be ruled by an island. The room laughs—then quiets.

By candlelight he writes *Common Sense*. We watch phrases tested, cut, and reset in type. The pamphlet spreads through markets, farms, workshops, and military camps; its words pass from one reader’s voice to another. Independence becomes imaginable. The act ends on July 4, 1776—not inside Independence Hall, but outside, where an illiterate laborer asks a girl to read the declaration aloud.

## Act II — The blaze (35–88 minutes)

The Revolution falters. Paine joins the retreat across New Jersey and sees bloody footprints in snow. Beside a weak fire he writes, “These are the times that try men’s souls.” Washington orders the essay read to the army before Trenton. Victory follows, but Paine receives little money and less stable authority. His talent makes him essential in crisis and intolerable in committee rooms.

After the war he designs an iron bridge and dreams of invention unconnected to faction. In England, outrage over Edmund Burke’s attack on the French Revolution draws him back to the pen. *Rights of Man* defends republican government and proposes education, pensions, and support for the poor. Huge crowds buy it. The Crown prosecutes him for seditious libel. Warned just ahead of arrest, Paine escapes across the Channel while officials seize his papers.

France welcomes him as a hero and elects him to the National Convention, though he barely speaks French. Revolution darkens. Paine votes to abolish monarchy but opposes executing Louis XVI, insisting that rights must apply to a defeated enemy. Robespierre’s Terror consumes nuance. Paine is arrested and confined in the Luxembourg.

Expecting execution, he races to complete *The Age of Reason*, arguing for God but against revealed religion and priestly power. In prison, fever overtakes him. A guard chalks the sign of death on his open door; when it closes, the mark faces inward and Paine is passed over. From his cell he hears carts carrying prisoners to the guillotine.

Robespierre falls. James Monroe secures Paine’s release, but Paine emerges furious that Washington did not protect him. He publishes a savage letter against the President and alienates former allies. His religious book reaches America ahead of him, where ministers recast the revolutionary voice as an atheist monster.

Napoleon visits, flatters Paine, and hints at invading England. Paine sees another crown forming beneath republican language and rejects him. A letter arrives from President Thomas Jefferson: come home. Paine stands at the rail of the westbound *London*, France disappearing into Atlantic gray.

## Act III — The ember (88–120 minutes)

Paine returns in 1802 to a country that knows his scandal better than his service. Jefferson welcomes him to the unfinished President’s House. Their affectionate dinner turns tense when enslaved hands serve men discussing liberty. Paine tells Jefferson the Revolution cannot be complete while slavery remains.

Federalist newspapers use Paine to attack Jefferson. Former friends avoid him. Veterans thank him for *The Crisis*, then retreat at the mention of *The Age of Reason*. At his New Rochelle farm, Marguerite de Bonneville and her sons provide an improvised family. Paine is funny with the children and impossible with everyone else. When election officials deny his right to vote on the claim that French citizenship erased his American citizenship, he asks, “Which of you became a citizen first?”

Age and gout confine him. Clergymen visit his deathbed seeking a recantation; he refuses to manufacture belief for their comfort. On June 8, 1809, Paine dies with only a few attendants. Quakers deny his request for burial in their cemetery. A tiny procession carries him to his farm, where six mourners stand beneath a walnut tree.

Ten years later, English radical William Cobbett—once Paine’s vicious enemy—secretly digs up the coffin, intending to build a monument in England. Lantern light reveals bone. The planned memorial fails, and after Cobbett’s death Paine’s remains disappear.

We return to the empty grave in New Rochelle. Wind moves through grass. Match cut to a modern hand opening *Common Sense* in a public library. Paine’s first lines sound in the new reader’s voice. The camera moves close to the page until paper grain becomes landscape.

The ink looks wet.
