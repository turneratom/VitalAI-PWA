# Chapter 9: The Return

The Atlantic had taken him west once as a man with a letter in his pocket and no country to lose. Now, in the autumn of 1802, it carried him back as a citizen of three revolutions and a welcome no one could measure.

Thomas Paine stood at the rail of the *London* and watched France diminish into gray. At sixty-five he had learned that coastlines did not vanish all at once. They thinned. Towers went first, then roofs, then the dark suggestion of earth, until a nation that had nearly killed you became only an argument with the horizon.

The wind worried his coat. Salt gathered in his hair. Behind him, in the cramped life of the packet ship, crockery knocked and timbers complained. Ahead lay America: the republic he had named before it knew its name, the experiment whose independence he had turned from tavern murmur into common speech. He had been absent fifteen years.

Fifteen years was long enough for a revolution to become a government and for gratitude to become partisan.

He carried Thomas Jefferson’s invitation among his papers. The President had written with the warmth of a man repaying an old intellectual debt, offering Paine passage on a national vessel if one could be arranged and assuring him that the country had not forgotten. Paine had read the letter until its folds softened. Jefferson, at least, remembered the winter soldier.

But even the invitation contained weather. America had changed while Paine sat in a French prison expecting death, while he argued against royal blood and revolutionary murder, while he wrote *The Age of Reason* with the Luxembourg’s key almost turning in its lock. In the United States, printers had lifted passages from that book, ministers had thundered against it, and political enemies had joined his name to infidelity as tightly as they had once joined it to independence.

The author of *Common Sense* was coming home.

So was the author of *The Age of Reason*.

America would decide which man stepped ashore.

The voyage lasted long enough for memory to become a second sea. On clear mornings Paine paced the deck and reconstructed Philadelphia as it had been: brick wet with winter, presses clattering, men bent over proofs by candlelight, Washington’s army leaving blood in the snow. He remembered the sentence that had traveled faster than any horse—*These are the times that try men’s souls*—and how urgently true it had seemed. It seemed true still, though now the trial was less glorious. Could a republic honor dissent after victory? Could men love liberty when it contradicted them?

A young passenger recognized him on the third week out. The fellow was a merchant’s clerk with neat cuffs and the guarded curiosity of someone approaching a dangerous animal.

“Mr. Paine?”

Paine turned.

“You are the Mr. Paine?”

“I have generally found one sufficient.”

The clerk laughed before deciding he ought not. “My father read *Common Sense* to the neighbors.”

“Then your father improved his neighbors.”

“He says you did great service.”

The past tense hung between them.

“And what do you say?”

The clerk looked toward the mast. “I have not read the other book.”

“Then you possess the rare advantage of ignorance honestly confessed.”

That ended the conversation, though not cruelly. The young man returned twice in the following days, asking about France, the prison, Bonaparte, and whether Paine truly disbelieved the Bible. Paine answered as he always had: too fully for comfort. He believed in one God, he said, and in no priest’s ownership of God. Revelation given to another man was hearsay to him. Cruelty did not become holy because set in type. The Creator’s work was the creation itself, open to every eye.

The clerk listened, troubled. “But why attack what consoles people?”

“A lie that consoles today may command a hanging tomorrow.”

“You make everything political.”

“Everything that claims obedience is political.”

The clerk did not return after that. Paine watched him avoid the rail and understood, without surrendering, the shape of the country waiting ahead.

Late in October the American shore rose from the water. It did not resemble the republic of Paine’s memory because no country ever resembles its idea from a distance. There were marshes, low trees, smoke, gulls shrieking over refuse, pilots shouting practical instructions. He felt no anthem inside him. He felt tired, grateful, and afraid.

News of his arrival traveled unevenly. In Baltimore, hands were offered and withdrawn. Old republicans called. Curious strangers stared. Newspaper editors who had once printed his words as ammunition now printed his name as scandal. Some welcomed “the immortal Paine.” Others announced the arrival of the blasphemer, the drunkard, the Jacobin, the moral pestilence imported from France.

Paine read the attacks over coffee and marked their errors in the margin.

“Why do you keep them?” a sympathizer asked.

“Because a man ought to know what crimes he has committed without attending.”

The caricatures amused him less. They gave him a bottle, a torn coat, a face swollen by appetite. They dressed theological disagreement as bodily corruption. It was an old trick: if a man’s argument could not be answered, stain his cuffs, sour his breath, and let respectability do the rest.

He was no saint of temperance. Illness and years had thickened him. His clothes often lost their argument with travel. He enjoyed brandy, spoke sharply, and treated ceremony as a species of fraud. Enemies took each human untidiness and inflated it until the private flaw eclipsed the public labor. America, which had once needed a plain man’s dangerous pen, now inspected whether that man used the correct fork.

Jefferson’s orbit drew him toward Washington. The new capital looked less like Rome than a construction site interrupted by politics. Mud governed the avenues. Buildings stood far apart in fields, pale assertions against emptiness. The President’s House, unfinished in spirit even where its walls were complete, seemed to Paine both grand and lonely.

Jefferson received him not in royal splendor but with the cultivated ease of a philosopher who had accidentally acquired servants, ceremony, and executive power. He was taller than Paine remembered, or perhaps office made every man appear elongated. The two regarded each other for a moment across all that had happened since Paris.

Then Jefferson came forward and took his hands.

“Welcome home.”

Paine searched his face for qualification and found none visible. “I was beginning to think the republic had changed the locks.”

“It has added too many doors.”

They dined and talked past the patience of others. The old subjects returned wearing new clothes: representative government, hereditary power, public education, religious freedom, France’s wounds, Britain’s endurance, America’s possibilities. Paine described the Luxembourg prison without embellishment because the facts had already exceeded drama. Jefferson listened with his long fingers joined beneath his chin.

“You came close,” he said.

“Close enough to learn that death has poor conversation.”

Paine told him of the chalk mark placed upon prison doors to signal the condemned. His own door had stood open when the mark was made; when closed, the fatal sign faced inward and was missed. Fever, chance, a hinge—history had preserved him through carpentry.

Jefferson looked toward the candle. “Providence.”

“Geometry.”

The President smiled. Neither pressed the point.

For a brief season Paine moved near power without belonging to it. He visited the President’s House, sat among officeholders who knew his reputation before they knew his face, and discovered that revolutionaries become awkward furniture in administrations. Jefferson’s friendship was genuine, but it was also costly. Federalist editors used Paine as a club against him: see whom the President honors; see what unbelief sits at his table. The invitation itself became evidence in the national prosecution of Jefferson’s character.

Some of Jefferson’s allies greeted Paine warmly in private and coolly where witnesses might remember. They praised *Common Sense* and changed the subject at *Rights of Man*. They praised *Rights of Man* and frowned at *The Age of Reason*. Each man wanted to rescue a useful portion of Paine from the whole, as if cutting pages from a book could produce a safer author.

Paine would not assist.

“My mind is not a boardinghouse,” he told one nervous congressman. “You cannot take the room you like and declare the rest unoccupied.”

The congressman had admired him since youth. Paine could see that admiration struggling against ambition and losing.

He also saw the contradiction at the heart of Jefferson’s republic. Enslaved people passed through the rooms where natural rights were discussed. Black hands carried wine to men debating human liberty. Paine, who had denounced slavery in the revolutionary years and never owned another human being, felt the old impatience turn bitter. A nation could speak universally and act locally; it could put equality in a declaration and bondage in the kitchen.

One evening he and Jefferson walked under a sky made enormous by the unfinished city. The ground sucked at their shoes. Somewhere beyond the darkness, laborers were still moving stone.

“We declared too much to remain as we are,” Paine said.

Jefferson understood. He often understood most clearly where he acted least decisively.

“Institutions yield slowly.”

“Men in chains do not experience slow yielding as philosophy.”

The President’s face closed by degrees. “You think I do not know the difficulty.”

“I think difficulty is the word power uses when conscience arrives.”

They walked farther without speaking. Friendship between thinkers can survive disagreement; friendship between a thinker and a president must survive consequence. Jefferson did not dismiss him. Paine did not soften. The mud kept both men honest by clinging equally to their boots.

Washington could not hold him. It was too ceremonial for his taste and too aware of reputation for his safety. He traveled north, following old names into altered places. Philadelphia had streets he remembered and faces he did not. The city’s political heart had shifted. Taverns where independence once sounded treasonous now hosted men boasting of how early they had supported it. Memory had become a competition in which the living awarded themselves good seats.

At one doorway an old acquaintance saw him, paused, and made a performance of failing to recognize him.

Paine stopped.

“You have improved your sight,” he said. “It now sees only what is useful.”

The man flushed. “Mr. Paine, I did not—”

“You did.”

Paine moved on before apology could reduce the truth.

Other meetings cut more quietly. Benjamin Rush, physician, reformer, signer, and once a man who had encouraged the title *Common Sense*, had recoiled from Paine’s religious writing. Their old warmth could not cross the theological breach. To Rush, revealed Christianity anchored virtue. To Paine, institutions that claimed revelation too easily licensed tyranny. Each believed the other had endangered the moral architecture of freedom.

What pained Paine was not disagreement. He had lived on disagreement. It was the revision of affection—the way men recast former intimacy as youthful error once the friendship became socially expensive. He could withstand being called wrong. Being treated as contaminating required a different armor.

In taverns, veterans sometimes approached him with shining eyes. One had carried *The Crisis* folded inside his coat at Trenton. Another remembered an officer reading it beside a fire while men wrapped rags around their feet. They thanked him with a force that embarrassed them both.

“I only wrote what the hour required,” Paine would say.

“The hour required someone to write it.”

Those encounters restored him. Not because they proved him famous, but because they returned words to their human destination. A pamphlet did not live in literary society. It lived in a cold man hearing a sentence and deciding to remain one more night.

Then the veteran would lower his voice.

“They say you deny our Savior.”

There it was again: gratitude stopped at the church door.

“They say more than they read,” Paine answered.

Sometimes the man stayed. Often he did not.

Paine began publishing letters to answer the campaign against him. He addressed citizens directly, as he always had, explaining his opinions, attacking priestcraft, defending republican principles, criticizing Washington’s later policies with a vehemence that shocked those who preferred heroes sealed in marble. The pen had lost none of its edge. If anything, isolation sharpened it.

But the public sphere was no longer the revolutionary clearing where one plain voice could define the question. It had hardened into parties and newspapers that manufactured entire climates of fact. Federalist presses described him as a French monster under presidential protection. Republican editors defended him cautiously or not at all. A sentence could cross the nation, but so could a slander, and slander traveled lighter.

Children learned his infamy before they learned his contribution. Boys shouted “Tom Paine!” as insult and sport when he passed. Adults repeated tales of filth and drunkenness improved through retelling. Clergymen made him a character in sermons: the apostate alone, abandoned, proof that unbelief decayed the body as well as the soul.

He answered ridicule with wit until wit no longer concealed the wound.

At a public house, a man loudly announced that no Christian should sit at Paine’s table. Paine looked at the empty chairs around him.

“Then Christianity has solved the crowding.”

The room laughed. The man left satisfied that laughter itself was persecution. Paine finished his meal without tasting it.

His refuge waited in New Rochelle: the farm the State of New York had granted him after the Revolution, land confiscated from a Loyalist and bestowed in recognition of service. The gift had always carried irony. Paine, enemy of inherited privilege, had become a landed gentleman by legislative gratitude. He accepted without pretending the acres had made him aristocratic.

The property lay north of New York City among fields, orchards, stone walls, and roads that became arguments in rain. The house was plain. The cottage was plainer. From certain rises he could see Long Island Sound holding the sky in a hard blue strip. Wind came across the land with no respect for reputation.

Here he tried to inhabit the word *home*.

He walked boundaries, considered repairs, quarreled over rents, measured the competence of caretakers, and discovered that agricultural life was less a pastoral ideal than an endless committee of mud, fences, weather, and unpaid accounts. He had ideas about bridges, iron, government, religion, constitutions, currencies, and now drainage. The mind that had designed an audacious single-span iron bridge bent itself to gates that would not latch.

Neighbors observed him. Some came from curiosity, some from kindness, some to test the famous infidel in debate. Paine welcomed argument more readily than pity. He could be charming when not required to be. He told stories of Franklin with mimicry, described French politicians with acid accuracy, and made children laugh by balancing a spoon upon his nose. Then a guest would question his honor or quote scripture as if issuing a warrant, and the evening would turn.

“You have read the denunciations,” Paine said to one visitor.

“I have read the Bible.”

“Have you read me?”

The visitor admitted he had not.

“Then one of us has come unarmed.”

He lent the man a copy of *The Age of Reason*. It was returned unopened.

The loneliness of New Rochelle was not absolute. He had friends, though fewer than legend later allowed and fewer than he needed. The family of Nicolas and Marguerite de Bonneville belonged to his late life with all the disorder of genuine attachment. Paine had known Nicolas in France, had shared political confidence and danger, and had helped Marguerite and her children come to America when Bonaparte’s regime pressed hard upon the family. Their fortunes became entangled with his property, his care, and the suspicions of others.

Marguerite possessed endurance without meekness. She could manage a household, endure gossip, and answer Paine’s moods without trembling. Her children brought noise into rooms that had begun to sound like mausoleums. With them Paine could briefly become Uncle Thomas rather than the Age of Infidelity made flesh.

He showed the boys models and sketches. He explained that an iron bridge worked not by defeating gravity but by persuading forces to distribute themselves. He might have been describing republican government.

“Everything bears weight,” he told them. “The question is whether one part is made to bear all of it.”

On winter evenings, candlelight gave the farmhouse a temporary mercy. Paine read newspapers and swore at errors. Marguerite mended. The children whispered and failed to whisper. Outside, branches scraped the roof like quills trying to write upon it.

Sometimes his talk returned to France. He spoke of Condorcet, of Danton, of the Girondins, of the National Convention, of the moment his vote against executing Louis XVI helped make him a target among men who confused blood with courage. He had opposed the king and opposed killing the king. To partisans, such distinctions looked like betrayal. To Paine, they were the substance of principle: if rights belonged only to agreeable men, they were privileges wearing a democratic cap.

“I fought monarchy,” he said. “I did not fight to make the scaffold king.”

The children did not understand all of it. Marguerite did.

His body kept account where his pride did not. The prison illness had left damage. Gout flared in his feet and hands, turning movement into negotiation. His face carried years heavily. He could still animate a room, but afterward he sagged as if speech had borrowed from reserves he could not replenish. There were mornings when getting from bed to chair became a campaign no newspaper would report.

He despised being managed. Offers of help sounded to him like claims of jurisdiction.

“Your coat,” Marguerite would say as he moved toward the door in cold weather.

“I have opposed stronger authorities than winter.”

“Winter has buried stronger men than you.”

He took the coat.

Paine’s stubbornness had once moved populations. In age it could make a household miserable. He quarreled over money, papers, property, and perceived slights. He accused friends too quickly and forgave too slowly. He had spent a lifetime teaching ordinary people to distrust authority and saw no reason to exempt those who told him to rest.

Yet the dignity beneath the obstinacy was real. He would not perform repentance to purchase company. He would not declare belief he did not hold so that Christians could enjoy saving him. He would not permit the men who had benefited from his political courage to recast his intellectual courage as senility. Poverty, ridicule, and pain could narrow his circumstances; they could not make him say that two and two were five.

The country tested that refusal in small humiliations.

In 1806, when Paine presented himself to vote in New Rochelle, officials denied him the ballot. The author who had argued America into independence was told his citizenship was doubtful. His election to the French National Convention, they suggested, had made him a French citizen and therefore not an American one.

The absurdity had a cruel precision. Paine had been born an English subject, helped create the United States, been honored by states for revolutionary service, narrowly escaped execution while an American ambassador failed to protect him, and now stood before local officials explaining why he belonged.

“If I am not a citizen,” he demanded, “which of you became one first?”

The answer was procedure.

Procedure is the language injustice uses when it does not wish to raise its voice.

Paine left without voting. The road back to the farm seemed longer than it had that morning. He had written that the right of voting for representatives was the primary right by which other rights were protected. Denial was not merely personal insult. It was an attack upon the architecture he had spent his life describing.

At home he drafted an account. His anger restored his energy. Every date mattered. Every service became evidence. He listed his arrival in America, his work in the cause, his positions, his return under Jefferson’s invitation. The page received what the polling place had refused.

Still, paper could not erase the image: Thomas Paine turned away from an American ballot box.

The republic had used his voice before deciding whether he had one.

His relationship to America became that of a disappointed parent who refuses to disown the child. He criticized its parties, its churches, its compromises, its appetite for commercial rank, its continuing slavery. But he never treated failure as proof that the experiment had been foolish. A republic was not sacred because founded; it was worth defending because unfinished.

Younger radicals came occasionally. Freethinkers, mechanics, printers, men with schemes for social reform. They arrived expecting either a prophet or a ruin and found a complicated old man who could be both before supper. Paine questioned them. What had they read? What did they propose? Who paid? Who benefited? Fine phrases irritated him unless attached to practical machinery.

One young admirer delivered a long speech on universal liberty.

“Good,” Paine said. “Now tell me how the widow buys bread.”

The admirer hesitated.

“Liberty that cannot survive the market is a toast.”

Paine still believed in provision for the poor, in education, in pensions for age, in public responsibility toward those whom property excluded. In *Agrarian Justice* he had argued that civilization created both wealth and dispossession and therefore owed compensation to those deprived of their natural inheritance. Men who celebrated him as the father of independence rarely mentioned these proposals. Political memory preferred his trumpet to his ledger.

He did not divide freedom into respectable and embarrassing compartments. Independence, religious inquiry, social provision, opposition to slavery, international republicanism—all arose from one premise: no human being entered the world saddled for another.

That premise made him magnificent.

It also made him difficult to invite to dinner.

Years contracted. The geography of his life shrank from continents to the triangle between New Rochelle, New York, and the households willing to receive him. He moved between farm and city as health, money, and companionship required. Each move displaced papers. Manuscripts accumulated in trunks and drawers. Letters went unanswered, then were answered with volcanic length.

In New York he could still find argument. The city was growing louder, richer, and more certain of its own importance. Carts battered the streets. Masts crowded the river. Churches lifted steeples over countinghouses, each institution confident it spoke nearest to heaven. Paine moved through the crowd slowly, recognized just often enough to be watched and not often enough to be helped.

Printers remained his natural clergy. In a print shop he relaxed among ink, metal, paper, and profanity. Type did not care whether its author wore a clean coat. A press judged an argument by whether it could be set, inked, pulled, and sold.

He visited one office where an apprentice, no more than sixteen, stared at him.

“You wrote *Common Sense*?”

“The printer did much of the physical labor.”

“My master says it changed everything.”

“Everything claims to have been changed. The question is what remains unchanged.”

The boy thought about that while sorting type. Paine watched his blackened fingers and saw his younger self under Joseph’s workbench: hands learning a trade while the mind escaped through language.

“Read those letters backward?” Paine asked.

The apprentice nodded.

“Useful practice for politics.”

The boy grinned. For that moment, reputation vanished. There were only two workers among tools.

Elsewhere, hostility became theatrical. A minister visited with witnesses to rescue Paine’s soul, though the arrangement seemed designed to advertise the rescuer. He began by asking whether Paine feared eternity.

“I fear men who presume to describe it in detail.”

“Sir, this levity—”

“Is my own. You have brought sufficient solemnity for both of us.”

The minister insisted that age had softened stronger skeptics. Paine asked whether truth changed with pulse rate. The visitors left reporting hardness of heart. Paine reported an interrupted afternoon.

But when alone, he did think about death. Not hell as the preachers painted it. He had seen men build hell efficiently on earth and label the work virtue. He thought instead about extinction, continuation, matter, the astonishing fact of consciousness. His deism did not offer the intimate assurances of evangelical faith. It offered order without biography: creation as evidence of a creator, mortality as a boundary reason could approach but not cross.

He was capable of doubt. What he refused was counterfeit certainty.

On the farm, he sometimes woke before dawn and sat at the window while the land emerged. First blackness, then outlines, then individual trees. The process comforted him because nothing needed a priest to occur. Light made no argument for itself. It arrived.

He thought of those who had disappeared: Franklin, whose calm intelligence had opened America to him; Washington, whom he had once praised as indispensable and later condemned as faithless; revolutionary friends dead in battle, fever, prison, or respectability; his parents beneath English ground; the republic’s anonymous soldiers. The dead formed the only assembly in which he could not demand the floor.

Regret visited without introducing itself. His letter attacking Washington had been written from injury and conviction, but injury had held the louder pen. His estrangements had causes, yet causes did not warm a room. He wondered whether truth always required the particular sharpness he gave it or whether sharpness had sometimes become habit—the sword kept swinging after the rope was cut.

Then a hostile newspaper arrived, and introspection fled before outrage.

He remained Thomas Paine.

One afternoon a former soldier came to New Rochelle carrying a battered copy of *The Crisis*. The pages had been sewn into a homemade cover. Smoke had browned the edges.

“This was at Valley Forge,” the man said.

Paine took it carefully. The object weighed almost nothing, yet his hands lowered as though receiving iron.

“Whose?”

“My brother’s. He did not come out.”

Paine opened to the first essay. The type was cramped, the paper cheap, the sentence familiar enough to have become public property. He remembered composing it amid retreat in 1776, with confidence scarce and catastrophe near. He had not known the brother. He had written to him anyway.

“I thought you should see what your words looked like after the war,” the soldier said.

The book’s stains defeated interpretation. Rain, snow, blood, coffee—history does not label its marks.

Paine returned it. “Keep him with you.”

“I came to thank you.”

“Then thank him for me.”

After the man left, Paine stood in the doorway a long time. The farm spread before him in late light. His own country had denied him a vote. Churches called him monster. Friends crossed streets. None of it could reach backward and remove that pamphlet from a dead soldier’s coat.

Public opinion is loud.

Usefulness is quiet and survives it.

The dignity Paine kept was not the polished dignity of portraits. It did not require graceful decline, forgiving speeches, or clean linen arranged by admiring descendants. His was the stubborn dignity of refusing to let rejection define the worth of the gift. America’s ingratitude might indict America. It did not annul independence.

He had returned expecting perhaps a republic mature enough to welcome argument. He found a country as anxious, tribal, devout, ambitious, and unfinished as the people who made it. The discovery hurt because the hope had been sincere.

Yet he did not sail away.

He stayed on American ground. He wrote. He quarreled. He received the few who came. He looked across fields given for service by a state now uncertain whether he belonged to it. In good weather he walked with a stick, stopping where the land fell toward the Sound. Ships moved in the distance, their sails briefly bright.

Once, the Atlantic had offered escape.

Now it marked the outer edge of return.

As evening settled, New Rochelle windows lit one by one. Families gathered behind glass. Somewhere a Bible opened. Somewhere a newspaper repeated a lie. Somewhere, perhaps, a mechanic unfolded an old pamphlet and found that its sentences still addressed him without bowing.

Paine turned toward the house slowly, anger and gratitude traveling together in the same worn body.

No crowd waited inside. No nation rose to applaud. There was a chair, a candle, a stack of papers, and enough ink for one more refusal.

He closed the door against the wind and sat down to write.
